\begin{abstract}
We asked whether a protein-interaction graph, a map of which proteins interact and on what evidence,
helps a computer model predict how human T cells respond when one gene is turned down. We compared four
models that differ only in how they use the graph. Two things went wrong. First, a standard training
penalty (a regularizer) silently shut every link in the graph. Within the first pass over the data it
pushed the links' gates, learned dials from $0$ (off) to $1$ (on), to about $10^{-7}$ on average, yet
training looked normal. Second, after we removed the penalty, our gated model still
could not be told apart from the model that does not read the graph (the difference in our score,
\textsc{systema}, was $-0.0009$, with a $95\%$ confidence interval of $[-0.0072,+0.0054]$; five runs per
model). A version with every link always on showed no benefit on seven more Perturb-seq datasets. Two
findings point to why. A simpler graph model beat the no-graph model on the T-cell data (it passed
correction for multiple tests only after we added two runs per model to the first five, so this result is
somewhat optimistic) and on one other dataset, but did worse on a third. So our negative result says more about how our model's encoder reads the graph than
about the graph itself. And the no-graph model was not free of the graph: removing the graph-derived
features among its inputs lowered its score by $0.0036$, a drop that survives correction, while the
gated graph had no detectable effect (the two effects do not differ significantly). When we planted a
known graph-shaped signal in the data, the gated model's difference from no graph failed correction at
every size we tried, so what this setup can detect also limits our result. We end with a checklist for
experiments that switch parts of a model on or off.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Perturb-seq experiments use CRISPR to turn down, knock out or switch on genes, usually one per cell, and
then measure how the expression of every other gene changes. Recent screens do this for nearly every
expressed gene in primary human cells \citep{dixit2016perturbseq,replogle2022genomescale,zhu2025gwperturbseq}.
We ask how well a model can predict the response to a gene it never saw during training.

Protein-interaction graphs record decades of evidence about which proteins work together. In such a
graph each protein is a point, and each known interaction is a link between two points, called an
\emph{edge}. A graph like this is a natural \emph{prior}: knowledge given to a model before training.
Does it help predict the response to an unseen gene? Published answers disagree (\S\ref{sec:related}).

We built a model to give the graph its best chance and compared it with the same model with its graph
reader switched off. Most of the project went into finding out that our measurement was broken. We
report four findings. First, a standard training penalty silently shut every edge, and the fix is one
line (\S\ref{sec:confound}). Second, with the edges open again, our gated model still did not beat the
no-graph model (\S\ref{sec:null}). Third, a simpler graph model points to our encoder as a likely cause
(\S\ref{sec:repl}, \S\ref{sec:causes}). Fourth, our ``no graph'' model still received a summary of the
graph through its input features (\S\ref{sec:causes}). We end with a checklist for similar experiments
(\S\ref{sec:checklist}). Appendix~\ref{app:glossary} explains the technical terms.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Benchmarks report that deep-learning and foundation models (large models pretrained on broad data) do not
beat simple baselines, such as a linear model or the average training response, at predicting these
responses \citep{ahlmanneltze2025linear,wongmoccia2025crispr}. Other studies report that graph and
foundation models do win when judged by their own ways of dividing genes into training and test sets, and
by their own scores \citep{wenkel2026txpert,cole2026foundation}. \textsc{gears} \citep{roohani2024gears}
and TxPert \citep{wenkel2026txpert} build gene-gene knowledge graphs into the predictor, the design we
test. Our model computes a gate for each edge from its evidence (\S\ref{sec:approach}), much like
differentiable edge masking
\citep{schlichtkrull2021graphmask}, which also learns gates on edges under a penalty that pushes them
toward zero.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Problem}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data.} We use the genome-scale Perturb-seq screen of primary human CD4$^{+}$ T cells of
\citet{zhu2025gwperturbseq}. It turns down one target gene per cell with CRISPR interference (CRISPRi), in
cells from four donors that were resting or stimulated for 8 or 48 hours. For each target and culture
condition, the \emph{response} is how much each gene's expression changed relative to unperturbed
control cells, as a \emph{z-score} published by the screen's authors and capped at ten either way. One example, or \emph{row}, is one (target,
condition) pair. The data hold $33{,}983$ rows covering $11{,}526$ targets, and each response is measured
over $10{,}282$ genes. The models predict the response as changes in $K{=}128$ \emph{programs}, groups
of genes that change together, found by sparse principal component analysis \citep{zou2006sparsepca} on
training targets only.

\paragraph{Score.} Our main score, \textsc{systema} \citep{vinas2025systema}, compares predicted and
observed responses one row at a time. For each held-out row we subtract the average training response
from both, compute the correlation between them across the programs, and then average over rows. Because
of that subtraction, a model gets no credit for predicting the generic response that many perturbations
share: predicting the average training response scores $0$, and $1$ is a perfect correlation. On the
T-cell screen the four models score between $0.0726$ and $0.0902$ (Table~\ref{tab:family}). These low
scores fit a screen with little target-specific signal (cause A in \S\ref{sec:causes}).

\paragraph{Split.} We divide the targets into \emph{training} targets, which the models learn from, and
\emph{validation} targets, which are held out and scored. The remaining targets are set aside, and no
result in this paper is scored on them. Genes with similar protein sequences can respond alike, so we
keep such genes on the same side of the split. We use ESM-2 \citep{lin2023esm2}, a model that turns a
protein's sequence into an \emph{embedding}, a list of numbers that summarizes the protein. Genes whose
embeddings are similar (centered cosine $\geq 0.85$) form a group, and members of the same protein
complex join one group where the size limit allows. No group may hold more than $5\%$ of targets, and
each group goes wholly to training or wholly to validation. This is imperfect: $24\%$ of validation
targets still have a close relative in training, against $60\%$ for a simple split that ignores
similarity.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Training involves random choices, such as the model's starting values, so the
same model trained twice gives slightly different scores. A \emph{seed} fixes these choices. We train
every model with the same five seeds on the same split and compare two models seed by seed (a
\emph{paired} comparison), so noise that both runs share cancels; $n$ is the number of paired seeds. Each
run trains for up to $20$ epochs, stops early if its validation loss has not improved for ten epochs, and
is scored at its best epoch. For each difference
between two models we report the mean over seeds, a $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) and a $p$-value from a
paired $t$-test: how often a difference at least this large would arise by chance if the two models were
equal.
Testing several comparisons at once raises the chance of a lucky result, so we adjust the $p$-values. A difference is
\emph{significant}, or \emph{passes correction}, only if its $p$-value stays below $0.05$ after the
Bonferroni adjustment; we also report the less strict Holm adjustment \citep{holm1979}. The intervals are
not adjusted, so one can exclude zero while the difference still fails correction. Our written plan,
fixed before any graph model was trained, named two of the four comparisons in \S\ref{sec:approach}:
typed against no graph and gated against typed (they are \emph{pre-registered}). We added the other two,
and the rule of correcting all four together as one \emph{family}, after seeing early results but before
training the gated runs that our results use. Where a result uses seven seeds instead of five, we say so.

\subsection{Proposed approach}
\label{sec:approach}

\paragraph{Graph.} The graph links proteins and protein complexes by about $8$ million edges. Each edge
has a confidence score and one of three types of evidence: physical binding (protein-protein interaction,
PPI) \citep{huttlin2021bioplex,luck2020huri,oughtred2021biogrid}, membership of the same complex
\citep{tsitsiridis2023corum}, or functional association \citep{szklarczyk2023string}, a link inferred mostly
from indirect evidence such as shared expression. Functional associations are $85\%$ of the edges and the
least certain type.

\paragraph{Model.} Our model describes each target only by fixed features of its protein, never by a label
for the gene itself, so it cannot memorize genes and must generalize to new ones. Its graph
\emph{encoder}, the part that reads the graph, looks at a neighborhood of up to $512$ proteins around the
target, chosen by a fixed rule. In three rounds of \emph{message passing}, each protein updates its
internal state from \emph{messages} that its neighbors send along the edges. Each message is multiplied by
a learned \emph{gate} between $0$ (off) and $1$ (on). The gate is computed for each evidence type from the
culture condition and the edge's evidence, such as its source and confidence score. It does not see the
proteins themselves, so two edges with the same evidence get the same gate in the same condition. A learned weight,
computed for each row, mixes the graph-based prediction of the programs with one that skips the graph;
fixing that weight at zero gives the no-graph model's program predictions exactly
(Appendix~\ref{app:arch}).

\paragraph{Four models.} The four models share everything except how they use the graph
(Table~\ref{tab:arms}). The \emph{no-graph} model skips the graph encoder. The \emph{typed} model reads
the graph, treats each evidence type separately and keeps every gate fixed at $1$. The \emph{gated} model
is our full model, with learned gates. The \emph{untyped} model is a standard graph network
\citep{kipf2017gcn} over all links between two proteins, whatever their evidence. It ignores evidence
types, has no gates, and scales
each message down by how many partners its two proteins have, where our encoder adds messages up without
scaling; it is also simpler in other ways (Appendix~\ref{app:arch}). Our main test is whether the gated
model beats the no-graph model. The other three comparisons are typed against no graph, gated against
typed, and untyped against no graph. The approach can only work if the graph carries information about a
gene's response that its protein's own features do not, and if the gates can tell useful evidence from
useless evidence.

\section{Results: observed outcome}
\label{sec:outcome}

\subsection{A default penalty shut every edge}
\label{sec:confound}

Our first five-seed comparison found the gated model no better than the no-graph model, and we recorded a
clean negative result. A later look at the gates showed the problem: their mean was near $10^{-7}$, so
almost nothing passed along the edges.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{lambda_sweep.pdf}
\caption{The default penalty shuts the gates. Mean edge gate during training for the default penalty
strength ($10^{-2}$), the weakest we tried ($10^{-5}$) and no penalty, on a log scale. A gate below
$10^{-3}$ (dashed line) is effectively off. This is a small run, one seed per setting for $22$ epochs on the first $512$ training and
$128$ validation rows, so the gates fall more slowly than in the full runs (\S\ref{sec:confound}). Across
the eight penalty strengths we tried (six are not drawn), those from $10^{-3}$ up are dead from the second epoch and
the weakest, $10^{-5}$, from the twelfth; none recovers. Without the penalty the gates end at $0.76$.
These gaps are far larger than anything a different seed changes.}
\label{fig:lambda}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Cause.} The training loss, the number that training lowers, includes a penalty on the gates,
meant to keep few edges switched on and to discount low-confidence edges (Eq.~\ref{eq:graphloss} in
Appendix~\ref{app:arch}). It adds up a term for every edge in every row's neighborhood, tens of thousands
of edges, but divides only by the number of rows in a batch. At its default strength, at the start of
training, it was therefore about $103$ times larger than the error on the predicted programs, the main part
of the loss. Size alone does not explain the collapse, though. Our optimizer, AdamW
\citep{loshchilov2019adamw}, scales each step to the recent size of that parameter's gradient, so even a
small push keeps moving a gate if nothing pushes back. The prediction error barely reached the gates, so
the penalty decided which way they moved, and it pushes every gate toward zero. In Figure~\ref{fig:lambda}, even a penalty a
thousand times weaker shut the gates, only later.

\paragraph{Why it is dangerous.} The mean gate fell to about $10^{-7}$ within the first epoch, in all five
seeds. Nothing looked wrong: training did not crash, warn or diverge, and it reported a plausible score.
But with every gate near zero, almost nothing passes along the edges, so the comparison no longer tests
message passing. (The model still pools the features of the proteins in each neighborhood, which the
graph chooses.) We had expected no benefit, so we believed the result.

\paragraph{Fix.} The fix that worked is one line: set the penalty to zero. Dividing the penalty by the
number of edges made it small but did not change its direction: in a pilot run, $71\%$ of the gates were
dead after one epoch and $98\%$ after two. Without the penalty, the gates stayed live, with a mean between
$0.27$ and $0.78$ in the checkpoints we scored. Yet the main comparison barely moved, from $-0.0019$ to
$-0.0009$ (\S\ref{sec:null}), so the broken penalty did not create the negative result. Apart from
describing the collapse, this before-and-after comparison is the only use we make of the dead-gate runs. We now check the mean gate
before reading any result and discard runs whose mean gate is below $10^{-3}$. The check takes three
minutes; a training run takes hours.

\subsection{With the edges open, the gated model still does not beat no graph}
\label{sec:null}

With working gates, the gated model still cannot be told apart from the no-graph model
(Table~\ref{tab:family}): the difference is $-0.0009$, its interval crosses zero, and it fails
correction. With seven seeds it is $-0.0036$, also not significant (Appendix~\ref{app:repl}). The typed
model, whose gates are all fixed at $1$, scores below the no-graph model, and this is
the only comparison in the family that passes correction. The untyped model has the highest mean score,
but at five seeds its gain over no graph fails correction (with seven seeds it passes; \S\ref{sec:repl}).

\paragraph{Other scores and splits.} Under a second score, Pearson-delta (the same correlation without
subtracting the average training response), the gated model is still no better than no graph
(Appendix~\ref{app:causes}). We also tried two stricter splits, which keep more similar genes apart; on
neither does the gated model pass correction. Its best showing is on the first, the intermediate split:
$+0.0082$, positive in all five seeds, with uncorrected $p{=}0.025$. Two re-draws of that split, made the
same way with a different random choice of groups, gave $+0.0026$ and $+0.0021$, and none of the three
passes correction (Appendix~\ref{app:folds}).

\paragraph{The typed model's shortfall did not hold on a harder split.} Our first significant result,
that the typed model is worse than no graph, did not hold on the harder split: there the difference is
$-0.0012$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0074,+0.0050]$, $p=0.61$, and one seed reverses sign. Nothing in the family
passes correction on that split (Appendix~\ref{app:folds}). So ``an always-on typed graph hurts'' holds on
one split only.

\subsection{Seven more datasets}
\label{sec:repl}

We repeated the comparisons on seven more Perturb-seq screens collected by scPerturb
\citep{peidli2024scperturb} (Table~\ref{tab:repl}). They cover four other cell types, genes turned down,
knocked out and switched on, and a hundredfold range in the number of targets. Each dataset gets its own
split, its own programs and four seeds per model, and is corrected for multiple tests on its own
(Appendix~\ref{app:repl}). Six of the seven have only one culture condition, so they cannot test gates
that change with the condition. On those six we did not train the gated model, and our plan made typed
against no graph the main comparison.

\paragraph{The typed graph shows no benefit.} Pooled over the seven new datasets, the typed graph is worth
$+0.0018$ \textsc{systema}, with a random-effects $95\%$ CI of $[-0.0028,+0.0065]$ (Appendix~\ref{app:repl}).
So the data rule out a large pooled benefit, but not one as large as the untyped model's gain on the
T-cell screen. Frangieh is the only new dataset with several conditions and the only one on which we trained
the gated model; it is too small to decide.

\paragraph{A simpler graph model points at our encoder.} The untyped model passes correction on three of
the eight datasets, and they disagree in sign. It beats the no-graph model on the T-cell screen
($+0.0043$ \textsc{systema} at $n{=}7$, all seven seeds positive, Bonferroni $0.027$ and Holm $0.020$) and
on Replogle RPE1 ($+0.0675$, all four seeds positive), but it loses on Norman ($-0.0790$; all four of its
seeds are negative and none was dropped). On the T-cell screen this needs seven seeds: we added two after
the five-seed result fell short of correction, which makes these $p$-values somewhat optimistic
(Appendix~\ref{app:repl}). The sign does not follow the cell type, or whether genes were turned down or
switched on. Norman also uses fewer programs, so we cannot cleanly separate its result from that
difference. Pooled over all eight datasets, the untyped gain is $+0.0091$ ($p{=}0.12$); over only the
five that use all $K{=}128$ programs, which leaves out Norman and the two Tian datasets, it is $+0.0141$
($p{=}0.0056$). Our plan did not pool the untyped comparison, and both estimates fail the Hartung-Knapp
adjustment for having few datasets ($p{=}0.48$ and $0.24$; Appendix~\ref{app:repl}). On RPE1, where the untyped model helps most, the typed model scores lower than no graph
($-0.0196$, not significant). So the same graph, read by a simpler encoder, changes predictions a lot,
for the better on two datasets and for the worse on one. This points at our encoder, not only at the
graph, as a cause of the negative result (cause C in \S\ref{sec:causes}).

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Reason for failure: five candidate causes}
\label{sec:causes}

our verdict. Causes C and D carry our argument, and neither is about biology: C concerns how our model
reads the graph, and D what the no-graph model is fed. Appendix~\ref{app:causes} gives the details.

\paragraph{C. Our encoder is badly designed.} We first recorded this cause as refuted, based on a search
over design variants whose single-seed runs could not resolve differences this small
(Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}). On our main split, running all four models with seven seeds shows where the
typed model's shortfall comes from: the typed encoder itself, which the gates repair only in part, not
significantly. Much of it seems to come from how messages are added up. The typed model sums the
messages of each edge type without scaling, so a protein with many partners receives a very large sum.
Scaling each message down by how many partners its two proteins have, similar to what the untyped model
does, recovers $79\%$ of the gap between the typed and untyped models (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}). That
gain has uncorrected $p{=}0.0245$: it was the only one of four planned variants that we ran, and corrected
over all four it would not pass. The repaired model still does not beat no graph. The typed model is also scored after
its first epoch in four of five seeds, so part of its shortfall may come from how it trains.
\textbf{Holds} on our main split; it best explains the negative result.

\paragraph{D. The ``no graph'' model was never graph-free.} The usual no-graph baseline turns message
passing off, but its fixed input features still carry information from the graph: three counts of each
protein's partners (physical, functional and complex) and a PINNACLE embedding \citep{li2024pinnacle}
learned from an interaction network. Setting these to zero lowers \textsc{systema} by $0.0036$, a drop that
survives correction, and nearly all of it comes from the three counts (Appendix~\ref{app:causes}). The drop
is small, about four percent of the no-graph model's $0.0857$; it passes correction largely because the
no-graph model varies little between seeds. The gated graph, the thing we set out to measure, has no
detectable effect. Whether the graph-derived features are worth \emph{more} than the gated graph is not
settled: in a post hoc test paired by seed, the features' effect minus the gated model's is $+0.0045$
($95\%$ CI $[-0.0012,+0.0101]$, $p{=}0.093$); it comes out ahead in all five seeds, but its interval
crosses zero. A comparison that calls a model ``no graph'' while feeding it a summary of the graph does
not measure what its name says. \textbf{Holds.}

\subsection{Boundary conditions}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{What our setup can detect.} A confidence interval from one split describes that split only. To
learn what our setup can detect, we planted a known signal that follows the graph: each target's response
gained a multiple $\delta$ of its graph neighbors' mean training response, and we retrained
(Appendix~\ref{app:floor}). The gated model's difference from no graph passed correction at no size up to
$0.40$ response standard deviations (a standard deviation is the typical spread of the responses). The
untyped model's passed at every size, down to the smallest we tried, $0.02$, but it was already $+0.0048$
ahead of no graph before anything was planted. Counting only what the planting added, a post hoc step,
neither model's gain passes correction. With a scrambled graph, where the planted signal no longer follows
the real neighbors, nothing passed. So our negative result describes what this encoder can detect with
this many seeds, not whether a protein-interaction prior helps.

\paragraph{Scope.} No new dataset is a T-cell screen, so if the graph's value were specific to T cells,
our replication could not show it (Appendix~\ref{app:repl}). Other groups report gains from graphs under
different splits and scores \citep{wenkel2026txpert,cole2026foundation}. Scored their ways, the gated
model still shows no benefit on the whole response, and the untyped model helps under TxPert's score and
hurts under \textsc{gears}'. On some groups of genes the gated model does pass correction: for the graph on
genes that change little, and against it on the twenty genes that change most, though that one passes
only when corrected over the twenty scores (Appendix~\ref{app:causes}). These scores are computed on single genes, where the graph models carry an
extra term of their own, so they are not pure tests of the graph (Appendix~\ref{app:arch}).

\subsection{Actionable takeaways: the checklist we wish we had used}
\label{sec:checklist}

For any experiment that switches parts of a model on or off:
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item Log the health of the part being tested, here the gates, at every epoch, not only the loss and the
score (\S\ref{sec:confound}).
\item Decide in advance when a run counts as broken, here a mean gate below $10^{-3}$, and do not interpret
broken runs.
\item Check which part of the loss decides how the tested part moves, not only how large each part is
(\S\ref{sec:confound}).
\item List the baseline's inputs and remove any that come from the thing being tested (\S\ref{sec:causes},
cause D).
\item Test the simplest version of the idea, with enough seeds (\S\ref{sec:repl}).
\item Plant a known signal, with a scrambled control, to learn what the setup can detect
(\S\ref{sec:limits}).
\item Correct for multiple comparisons, and repeat any result that passes on a new or re-drawn split
(\S\ref{sec:null}, Appendix~\ref{app:folds}).
\item Check the result under more than one score (Appendix~\ref{app:causes}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We asked whether a protein-interaction graph helps predict how T cells respond to a turned-down gene. The
harder problem turned out to be trusting our own measurement: a standard penalty had shut the very edges
we were testing. With the gates repaired, our gated model could not be told apart from the model without
the graph, and the typed graph shows no benefit on seven more datasets. The one comparison that passed
correction at five seeds, the typed model's shortfall, did not hold on a harder split. A simpler graph
model helps on two datasets and hurts on one (on the T-cell screen only after we added two seeds), a sign
we cannot predict. So
our negative result says more about our encoder than about the graph. Two lessons carry over to other
work: strip the graph-derived features from the ``no graph'' baseline, and test the simplest way of using
a prior before concluding that the prior does not help.

\section{Model details}
\label{app:arch}

\paragraph{Inputs.} Each target protein is described by fixed features only: an ESM-2 protein-sequence
embedding \citep{lin2023esm2} ($1280$ numbers), a PINNACLE embedding \citep{li2024pinnacle} ($128$
numbers), three counts of its interaction partners, and the gene's baseline expression in control cells.
These are combined with the culture condition, the donor's control-cell profile and two covariates known
before any response is seen (such as the number of guide RNAs) into one vector of $256$ numbers. The
no-graph model uses this vector alone, so it too receives the graph-derived counts and embedding (cause D
in \S\ref{sec:causes}).

\paragraph{Graph encoder.} Around each target we take a neighborhood of at most $512$ proteins, up to two
steps away: physical and complex partners first, then functional partners by confidence score. We run
three rounds of message passing with separate weights for each evidence type, as in R-GCN
\citep{schlichtkrull2018rgcn} but without its scaling: each type's messages are summed. Each message is
signed, so it can push a protein's state up or down, and includes a term for the edge's evidence. The gate
on an edge from $u$ to $v$ of type $r$ is
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{u\to v}^{r}(c)=\sigma\!\big(w_{\mathrm{gate}}^{r}\cdot[\,h_{\mathrm{cond}}(c)\,\|\,f_e\,]+b_{\mathrm{gate}}^{r}\big)\in[0,1],
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
where $h_{\mathrm{cond}}(c)$ represents the culture condition, $f_e$ holds the edge's evidence features
(its source, confidence score, whether the binding is direct, and how many sources support it) and
$\sigma$ maps any number into $[0,1]$. The gate does not depend on the proteins, so it is computed once and
used in all three rounds. It multiplies the edge's message before the messages arriving at a protein are
summed. An attention step then pools the proteins' states into one vector for the target.

\paragraph{The untyped model.} It replaces these rounds with three layers of a standard graph convolution
\citep{kipf2017gcn} over all links between two proteins (physical binding, shared complex and
functional association), treated as one type. Each message is scaled by $1/\sqrt{d_i d_j}$, where $d_i$
and $d_j$ are the numbers of partners, within the neighborhood and plus one, of the two proteins it
joins. It has
no gates, no signed messages, no evidence term, no complex nodes and a simpler update.

\paragraph{The penalty.} The penalty on the gates is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{graph}}=\tfrac{1}{B}\sum_{r,b,e}\big(|\alpha^{r}_{b,e}| + (1-\mathrm{conf}^{r}_{b,e})(\alpha^{r}_{b,e})^2\big),
\label{eq:graphloss}
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over every edge $e$ of every type $r$ in the neighborhood of each of the $B$ rows in a
batch, and $\mathrm{conf}^{r}_{b,e}$ is the edge's confidence. It is divided by $B$ but not by the number
of edges, which is why it grew so large (\S\ref{sec:confound}).

\paragraph{Output.} The model makes two predictions of the program changes, one from the graph encoder and
one that skips it, and mixes them with a weight between $0$ and $1$ that it computes for each row. Fixing
that weight at zero gives the no-graph model's program predictions exactly, so the four models are versions
of one architecture. The graph models also add a graph-based correction to each gene's predicted change,
which the no-graph model lacks. \textsc{systema} scores the programs, so it does not read this term
directly, though the term is trained with the rest of the model and may shape the program predictions.
Scores computed on single genes (Appendix~\ref{app:causes}) read it directly.
After training, a separate module in the GNNExplainer tradition \citep{ying2019gnnexplainer} picks out the
few edges behind each prediction, its \emph{rationale}.

\section{Why the typed model does worse}
\label{app:mechanism}

With seven seeds on our main split, the typed model scores $-0.0120$ against no graph, worse in all seven
seeds, and this shortfall survives correction. Two explanations are tangled in that number: splitting the
edges by type may be the wrong design, or the typed model may simply have more parameters. Two extra
models, each registered with its decision rule before training, separate them ($n{=}5$, corrected as a
family of two). One shares a single message module across all edge types, which cuts the model's
parameters by a third; it moves \textsc{systema} by $+0.0004$ against the typed model ($95\%$ CI
$[-0.0048,+0.0057]$). The other gives every protein-protein edge a random type, keeping each type's edge
count; it moves it by $+0.0065$ ($[-0.0017,+0.0147]$). Neither passes correction. So the extra parameters
explain little of the shortfall (at most about two fifths, by the interval), and random labels recover
about half of it, too uncertain to rule the labels in or out.

The architecture search we first used to dismiss this cause could not have resolved it. Its $14$ runs used
one seed and five epochs each. If $14$ runs differed only by seed, we would expect their best and worst
scores to differ by about $0.0147$. The search's whole range, $0.0089$, was narrower than that, and about
half the $0.0176$ gap between the untyped and typed models.

\paragraph{Scaling the messages.} The typed model sums each edge type's messages. A variant that instead
weights each message by $1/\sqrt{d_i d_j}$, where $d_i$ and $d_j$ count each protein's partners of that
edge type in the neighborhood, gains $+0.0139$ \textsc{systema} over the typed model ($95\%$ CI
$[+0.0029,+0.0248]$, five of five seeds) and so recovers $79\%$ of the gap, with a wide interval. This
scaling is close to the untyped model's, which counts partners over all types together. We planned four
such message variants and ran only this one; the other three were dropped because too little of the gap
was left for them to detect. Alone it is a family of one, so its relevant number is the uncorrected
$p{=}0.0245$, which would not pass Bonferroni over all four ($0.098$). The repaired
model is still no better than no graph ($+0.0008$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0047,+0.0062]$). Most of the typed
model's shortfall therefore seems to come from the plain sum, under which a protein's update grows with
its number of partners.

\paragraph{When each model is scored.} Every run is scored at the epoch with its lowest validation loss.
Counting epochs from $1$, the no-graph and untyped runs reach theirs at epochs $15$ to $20$ and $14$ to
$20$, and all of them run to the $20$-epoch limit. The typed and gated runs peak within their first four
epochs and stop early; the typed model peaks at its very first epoch in four of five seeds. Longer
training could therefore change the no-graph and untyped runs, and with them every comparison against no
graph, including the main one. We did not test this, or choosing each run's best epoch by score instead of
loss.

\section{The replication}
\label{app:repl}

We pre-registered the replication before running it: the comparisons and their correction, four seeds per
model, the main comparison for each dataset, and the rules for pooling. We amended the plan three times,
and each amendment states that it was written before any replication model was trained. One changed how
responses are built: as first written, that rule kept only $12$ of Replogle RPE1's $2{,}393$ targets, by a
standard that our own screen's published responses do not meet. We built ten
public datasets in our format. Eight passed a quality check that perturbing a gene consistently changes
that gene's own expression. The two that failed, Datlinger and Shifrut, were the only T-cell screens among
the candidates, so the replication covers four cell types but not T cells. Of the eight that passed,
Papalexi has too few targets for a split, and the other seven were trained to completion
(Table~\ref{tab:repl}). The number of programs $K$ cannot exceed the number of training rows, so the three
smallest datasets use fewer programs, each number fixed before the first run.

\paragraph{Pooling.} Pooled over the seven new datasets, the typed graph's $+0.0018$ has a random-effects
$95\%$ CI of $[-0.0028,+0.0065]$ and a fixed-effect CI of $[-0.0005,+0.0040]$. The pre-registration pools
no untyped comparison, so the following two are post hoc. Over all eight datasets the untyped comparison is
$+0.0091$ ($p{=}0.12$); over the five datasets with $K{=}128$ it is $+0.0141$ ($[+0.0041,+0.0240]$,
$p{=}0.0056$). The Hartung-Knapp adjustment, which corrects a pooled interval for having few datasets,
raises these to $p{=}0.48$ and $p{=}0.24$, and widens the typed interval to $[-0.0053,+0.0089]$.

\paragraph{RPE1 and Frangieh.} On RPE1 the untyped gain has $95\%$ CI $[+0.0316,+0.1033]$ ($p{=}0.009$,
Bonferroni and Holm both $0.019$ in that dataset's family of two). On Frangieh, the gated model against no
graph is $+0.0033$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.0829,+0.0894]$), with every gate live throughout training: an interval
too wide to decide anything.

\paragraph{The extra seeds on the T-cell screen.} At five seeds the untyped comparison was $+0.0045$
($95\%$ CI $[+0.0011,+0.0079]$, Holm $0.062$ and Bonferroni $0.083$, clearing neither). After that result
we ran two more seeds (numbered $5$ and $6$) of the untyped and no-graph models, and then of the typed and
gated models. At
seven seeds, none dropped, the estimate is $+0.0043$ and it passes both corrections. Adding seeds after
seeing a near miss makes the seven-seed $p$-values somewhat optimistic. At seven seeds the gated model
against no graph is $-0.0036$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.0093,+0.0022]$); our main test stays at five seeds.

\section{Splits}
\label{app:folds}

\paragraph{Stricter splits.} Two stricter splits lower the similarity threshold and raise the group cap:
$0.80$ with $10\%$ (intermediate) and $0.75$ with $15\%$ (harder). With five paired seeds each, the gated
model against no graph is $-0.0009$, $+0.0082$ and $+0.0005$ on the main, intermediate and harder splits,
and none passes correction (Bonferroni $1.000$, $0.101$ and $1.000$). The typed model's shortfall is
$-0.0131$, $-0.0122$ and $-0.0012$. On the intermediate split it passes no correction ($p=0.23$) because
the typed model's spread across seeds is $0.0169$ there, against $0.0049$ on the main split; on the harder
split the shortfall itself disappears.

\paragraph{Re-drawing one split.} Changing only the random number that decides which groups go to training
or validation, three draws of the intermediate split give the gated model $+0.0082$ ($p=0.025$),
$+0.0026$ ($p=0.027$) and $+0.0021$ ($p=0.266$), all uncorrected. The experiment is the same, yet the
estimate spans fourfold: two draws would look like a benefit without correction and the third would not.
None passes Bonferroni over the family of four ($0.101$, $0.109$, $1.000$). This is why the corrected
verdict carries the paper, and why a sweep over splits needs several draws per setting.

\section{The five causes in more detail}
\label{app:causes}

\paragraph{A. Little signal.} Centroid accuracy, the fraction of held-out responses whose prediction is
closer to their own observed response than to any other, is about $0.001$ for every model: about one hit
in a thousand. An independent benchmark reports this
screen as one where models barely beat the average response \citep{royer2026tfm}. If there is little
target-specific signal to recover, no prior can add much.

\paragraph{B. The score.} \textsc{systema} gives no credit for predicting the average response, so it could
hide a real gain. Pearson-delta, the same correlation without that subtraction, varies less between seeds,
so it can detect smaller differences. Under it the four differences are $-0.0067$ (typed $-$ no graph),
$+0.0062$ (gated $-$ typed), $+0.0023$ (untyped $-$ no graph) and $-0.0005$ (gated $-$ no graph, $p=0.64$);
the first three pass both corrections and the last does not. Program cosine similarity, which compares the
direction of the predicted and observed program changes, agrees ($-0.0005$, $p=0.65$). So the score can
hide a benefit, but not a benefit of the gated model over no graph.

We also re-scored all four models under five scores that other groups report
\citep{roohani2024gears,wenkel2026txpert,peidli2024scperturb}, correcting over all twenty tests. These
scores use single genes, so they also reflect the graph models' extra gene-level term
(Appendix~\ref{app:arch}). The gated model passes correction in the graph's favor under none of them. The
untyped comparison flips sign: it is positive under TxPert's all-gene Pearson-delta ($+0.0089$) and
negative under \textsc{gears}' correlation over each perturbation's twenty most-changed genes ($-0.0424$),
both significant. Ranking genes by how much they change shows why: the untyped graph helps on most genes
but hurts on the twenty that change most. The gated model has the same shape. We grouped genes in two
ways, both planned in advance. Over nine groups ($36$ tests) it passes correction \emph{for} the graph only
on the two groups of genes that change least ($+0.0078$ and $+0.0064$, five of five seeds each, Bonferroni
$0.049$ and $0.024$); over ten equal groups ($40$ tests) it passes on five, none among the four that change
most. Its shortfall on the twenty genes that change most ($-0.0420$) passes correction over the twenty scores
but not over the finer gene groups (Bonferroni $0.062$). These are secondary tests and the
whole-response result stays negative, so we report them beside the negative result, not instead of it.

\paragraph{D. The graph-derived features.} The three counts give each protein's numbers of physical,
functional and complex partners; the physical and functional counts are nonzero for $96.5\%$ and $98.4\%$
of rows. The PINNACLE embedding is present for only $9.2\%$ of rows, because its CD4 T-cell context covers
$1{,}119$ proteins; the other $90.8\%$ get zeros. With the input size unchanged, zeroing the counts and the
embedding together changes \textsc{systema} by $-0.00357$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.00427,-0.00286]$), zeroing the
counts alone by $-0.00347$ ($[-0.00403,-0.00290]$), and zeroing PINNACLE alone by $+0.000006$. Corrected
over these three versions, the $p$-values are $0.0005$, $0.0002$ and $0.64$. Nearly all of the effect comes
from the three counts.

\paragraph{E. The edges.} In an audit with live gates, removing every functional-association edge changes
the predicted program response the most ($0.365$, against at most $0.0021$ for BioPlex, HuRI and CORUM),
largely because this type holds most of the edges: per $1\%$ of edges removed, it changes the prediction by
$0.0043$, against $0.0025$ for HuRI and $0.0014$ for BioPlex. A prediction made from its rationale alone,
the few edges picked out for it, is slightly farther from the full prediction than one made from the same
number of random edges.

\section{The planted-signal test}
\label{app:floor}

For each target, the planted signal is the mean training response of its graph neighbors, scaled to the
spread of the training responses and multiplied by $\delta$, so $\delta$ is a fraction of a response
standard deviation. No validation response enters it. A model that reads the graph can then predict a
held-out target from its neighbors. In a control step, each target instead gets another target's
neighbors at the largest $\delta$, which keeps the signal's size but breaks its link to the graph. We ran
six steps, $\delta=0.02$, $0.05$, $0.10$, $0.20$ and $0.40$ plus the control, with four paired seeds
each, corrected as one family of six.

The untyped model's difference from no graph passes correction at every planted size, from $0.02$
($+0.0088$, Bonferroni $0.003$), and not in the control ($-0.0001$). The gated model's passes at none: even
at $0.40$ it is $+0.0045$ (Bonferroni $1.000$). All $24$ gated runs kept live gates (minimum mean
$0.0240$). Both models start with a gap at $\delta=0$ (four seeds): the untyped model is already $+0.0048$
ahead of no graph, the gated model $0.0009$ behind. Subtracting each model's own starting gap, a post hoc
step, leaves what the planting added, and there neither passes correction: at $0.40$ the untyped model
adds $+0.0135$ ($p{=}0.032$) and the gated model $+0.0053$ ($p{=}0.035$), both uncorrected. One limit
applies: the planted signal is a neighbor mean, the shape a message-passing model is built to find, and a
real biological effect need not look like that.

\section{Glossary}
\label{app:glossary}

\paragraph{Biology.} \emph{T cell}: an immune cell; \emph{CD4$^{+}$} or helper T cells coordinate immune
responses. \emph{Primary cells}: cells taken directly from a donor, as opposed to a \emph{cell line} grown
in culture, such as K562 (a leukemia line) or RPE1 (retinal cells). \emph{Stimulation}: activating T cells
in culture, as an infection would. \emph{Expression}: how much RNA a cell makes from a gene.
\emph{Perturb-seq}: an experiment that perturbs genes, usually one per cell, and reads out every gene's
expression in that cell. \emph{z-score}: a change divided by its uncertainty, so a large z-score is a
change unlikely to be noise. \emph{CRISPRi} turns a gene down, \emph{CRISPRa} switches it on, and a
\emph{knockout} disrupts it. \emph{Target}: the perturbed gene. \emph{Response}: the change in every gene's
expression against unperturbed control cells. \emph{PPI}: protein-protein interaction. \emph{Protein
complex}: proteins that bind together to act as one unit. \emph{Functional association}: a link inferred
mostly from indirect evidence, such as shared expression patterns.

\paragraph{Models and training.} \emph{Graph}: the network of protein interactions given to the model.
\emph{Edge}: a link between two proteins in the graph, with the evidence behind it. \emph{Neighborhood}: the
proteins near a target in the graph that the encoder reads. \emph{Prior}: knowledge given to a model before
training. \emph{Encoder}: the part of a model that turns its inputs into numbers; here, the part that reads
the graph. \emph{Embedding}: a list of numbers that represents an object, such as a protein. \emph{Message
passing}: each protein updates its internal state from its graph neighbors' states. \emph{Evidence type}:
the kind of evidence behind an edge. \emph{Gate}: a learned number between $0$ and $1$ that scales the
messages on one edge; $0$ switches the edge off. A gate is \emph{dead} below $10^{-3}$; a run's gates are
dead when their mean is, and \emph{live} otherwise. \emph{Programs}: groups of genes that change together; the models predict
changes in these, and $K$ is their number. \emph{Row}: one (target, condition) response, one example for
the model. \emph{Baseline}: a simple model that others are compared with. \emph{Loss}: the number that
training lowers. \emph{Penalty}, or \emph{regularizer}: an extra term in the loss that favors some
property, such as few active edges. \emph{Parameter}: a number in the model that training adjusts.
\emph{Batch}: the few rows used in one training step. \emph{Epoch}: one pass over the training data.
\emph{Checkpoint}: a saved copy of a model during training. \emph{Rationale}: the few edges picked out to
explain a prediction.

\paragraph{Evaluation and statistics.} \emph{\textsc{systema}}: the correlation between predicted and
observed responses after the average training response is subtracted from both. \emph{Pearson-delta}: the
same correlation without that subtraction. \emph{Centroid accuracy}: the fraction of held-out responses
whose prediction is closer to their own observed response than to any other. \emph{Split}: the division of
targets into training and validation; a \emph{re-draw} repeats a split's recipe with a new random choice.
\emph{Seed}: the random number that fixes one training run's random choices. \emph{Paired}: two models
compared on the same seed. \emph{Confidence interval (CI)}: a range of values consistent with the data; one
that crosses zero means no detectable difference. \emph{Standard deviation}: the typical spread of a set of
numbers. \emph{$p$-value}: how often a difference at least this large would arise by chance if there were
none; \emph{uncorrected} means before correction. \emph{Bonferroni and Holm corrections}: two ways of
adjusting $p$-values when several comparisons are tested at once; Bonferroni is the stricter.
\emph{Significant}, or \emph{passes correction}: survives the Bonferroni correction (\S\ref{sec:setup}).
\emph{Family}: a set of comparisons corrected together. \emph{Pre-registration}: a written plan of the
comparisons and decision rules, fixed before the runs it covers. \emph{Post hoc}: an analysis chosen after
seeing the data. \emph{Pooling}: combining one comparison across datasets; a \emph{fixed-effect} pool
assumes one true effect, a \emph{random-effects} pool lets it differ between datasets, and the
\emph{Hartung-Knapp} adjustment corrects a random-effects interval for having few datasets.
