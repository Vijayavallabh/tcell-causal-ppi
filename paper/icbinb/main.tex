\begin{abstract}
We asked whether a protein-interaction graph, a map of which proteins interact and on what kind of
evidence, helps a model predict how primary human T cells respond when one gene is knocked down. Our
four models differ only in how they read the graph and are scored on held-out genes with \textsc{systema},
a correlation between predicted and observed responses ($0.072$ to $0.090$ on our screen). We report
two failures. First, a textbook penalty on the graph's edge gates, summed over every edge of each sampled
neighborhood without normalization, was about $103\times$ the main prediction-error term at its default weight and
drove every gate to $\sim$$10^{-7}$ within the first pass over the data, in all five runs; training still
reported a plausible score, though no messages passed along the edges. Second, with working
gates the gated graph model is indistinguishable from the no-graph baseline
($\Delta\,\textsc{systema}=-0.0009$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0072,+0.0054]$, $n{=}5$; not significant after
correction for multiple tests), the same model with every edge always on shows no benefit on seven more
Perturb-seq screens, and the only comparison in our pre-registered five-run family to pass correction (that always-on
model doing worse than no graph) failed to replicate on a harder split. Of five candidate causes, the two that carry
our argument concern our encoder, the part of the model that reads the graph, and our baseline, not
biology. The baseline was never graph-free: zeroing its graph-derived input features lowers
\textsc{systema} by $0.0036$, surviving correction, while the gated message passing over eight million
typed edges that we set out to measure has no detectable effect (the two do not differ significantly). A
simpler graph model without edge types or gates, run as a diagnostic, beats the baseline on our screen ($+0.0043$, $n{=}7$,
surviving Bonferroni and Holm) and on Replogle RPE1 ($+0.0675$) but \emph{loses} on Norman ($-0.0790$),
so three of eight datasets pass correction, in opposite directions. Our negative result is therefore
evidence about the encoder we built, not against the graph: a negative graph-versus-no-graph comparison
can be an artifact of the encoder. It is also limited by detection: with a known
graph-shaped signal added to the data, the gated model's difference from the baseline passes correction
at no size up to $0.40$ response standard deviations and the simpler model's from $0.02$, though neither
model's gain over its own result without the signal passes. We close with the checklist we wish we had
used.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Perturb-seq experiments perturb genes, usually one per cell and with CRISPR (knocking a gene down,
knocking it out or switching it on), and measure how every other gene's expression changes; genome-scale screens now do
this for nearly every expressed gene in primary human cells
\citep{dixit2016perturbseq,replogle2022genomescale,zhu2025gwperturbseq}. We ask how well a model predicts
that response for a target gene it never saw in training. Protein-interaction graphs, networks of
proteins joined by known interactions, hold decades of curated evidence about which proteins work
together: a natural \emph{prior}, knowledge given to a model before training. Does the graph help
predict the response to a held-out gene? Published answers disagree (\S\ref{sec:related}).

We built a model to give the graph its best chance: an \emph{encoder}, the part of the model that reads
the graph, that keeps each interaction's type of evidence and learns for every edge a gate, a weight
between $0$ and $1$ that sets how much the edge counts (Appendix~\ref{app:arch}, Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). We tested it as a nested
series of models, with pre-registered comparisons, paired runs and correction for multiple tests, and spent
most of the project discovering that our measurement was broken. We report a standard
penalty that silently switched off the mechanism under test, with its signature and a one-line fix
(\S\ref{sec:confound}); a repaired comparison in which the graph still did not help and the only
pre-registered comparison to pass correction at five seeds failed to replicate (\S\ref{sec:null}); evidence from a
simpler model, run all along, that points to our encoder as the cause (\S\ref{sec:repl},
\S\ref{sec:causes}); and a checklist for experiments that gate or remove model structure
(\S\ref{sec:checklist}). Appendix~\ref{app:glossary} defines the technical terms we use.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Benchmarks report that deep-learning and foundation models (large models pretrained on broad data) do
not beat simple baselines such as a linear model or the average training response
\citep{ahlmanneltze2025linear,wongmoccia2025crispr}, and that tabular foundation models match
specialized architectures \citep{hollmann2025tabpfn,royer2026tfm}; other work reports that graph and
foundation models win under its own splits and scores \citep{wenkel2026txpert,cole2026foundation}. The
disagreement tracks how test sets are built and scored more than model design. \textsc{gears}
\citep{roohani2024gears} and TxPert \citep{wenkel2026txpert} build gene-gene knowledge graphs into the
predictor, the design choice we test. scPerturb \citep{peidli2024scperturb} collects public
screens in one format, and Appendix~\ref{app:metrics} re-scores our results under five other scores. Our
condition gate is closest to differentiable edge masking \citep{schlichtkrull2021graphmask}, which also
learns a gate for each edge under a sparsity penalty, so the failure of \S\ref{sec:confound} is a hazard
of that construction in general, not of our model alone. Appendix~\ref{app:arch} places our encoders
among standard graph networks.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Problem}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Task and data.} The data are the genome-scale Perturb-seq screen of primary human
CD4$^{+}$ T cells of \citet{zhu2025gwperturbseq}, which knocks down one target gene per cell with CRISPR
interference (CRISPRi), in cells from four donors cultured resting or 8 or 48 hours after stimulation
(Rest, Stim8hr, Stim48hr). For each target and condition, the response is the change in every gene's
expression against unperturbed control cells, a differential-expression (DE) profile. One example, or
\emph{row}, is one (target, condition) pair: the model sees fixed features of the target's protein and
the condition, and predicts the response as changes in $K{=}128$ \emph{programs}, groups of genes that
change together, found by sparse principal component analysis \citep{zou2006sparsepca} on each split's
training targets only. The data hold $33{,}983$ such rows over $10{,}282$ genes, covering $11{,}526$
distinct targets.

\paragraph{Score and split.} The main score, \textsc{systema} \citep{vinas2025systema}, is computed on
the programs: it subtracts the average training response from each held-out prediction and observation
and correlates what is left, so a model earns no credit for predicting the generic response many
perturbations share; $0$ means no target-specific signal and $1$ a perfect correlation. A \emph{split}
divides the targets into training targets, which the model learns from, and validation targets, which
are held out and scored; a third set is held back and never scored
(Appendix~\ref{app:folds}). Because paralogs, genes related by duplication that often have similar
sequences, can respond alike, we group targets whose protein sequences look alike to the protein
language model ESM-2 \citep{lin2023esm2} (centered cosine similarity of their embeddings $\geq 0.85$,
groups capped at $5\%$ of targets) and keep each group whole, in training or in validation. The blocking
is imperfect, which leaves $24\%$ of validation targets with a close training paralog, against $60\%$
for a naive split (Appendix~\ref{app:folds}). Every transform learned from the responses, such as the
programs, is fit on training targets only.

\paragraph{Statistical protocol.} Training involves random choices, such as the starting weights and the
order of the rows, so the same model trained twice gives slightly different results; a \emph{seed}
fixes those choices. Every model is trained with the same five seeds on the same
\emph{frozen} split, the one every main result uses, and two models trained with the same seed are compared
with each other (\emph{paired}), so run-to-run noise they share cancels; $n$ counts these paired seeds.
Each run stops once its loss on the validation targets stops improving, and its best state is scored. A difference between two models is reported as its mean over seeds with a $95\%$ confidence
interval (CI); an interval that crosses zero means the data cannot tell the models apart. Four
comparisons were \emph{pre-registered} (Table~\ref{tab:arms}) and form one \emph{family}, the set of
tests corrected together: testing several at once raises the chance of a lucky hit, so each must pass
\emph{both} the Bonferroni and the Holm correction \citep{holm1979}. A claimed benefit must also be
positive on average over at least four paired seeds ($n\geq4$). After the five-seed untyped comparison,
positive in every seed, failed Bonferroni, we ran the untyped and no-graph models at two more seeds,
then the typed and gated models at the same seeds so that all four share seven (Appendix~\ref{app:repl});
we say wherever seven are used. The
results rest on $379$ training runs (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\subsection{Proposed approach}
\label{sec:approach}

\paragraph{Graph.} Nodes are proteins and protein complexes, and each target gene enters as its
protein. They are joined by $\sim$$8$M edges, each with a confidence score and one of three evidence
types: physical protein-protein interaction (PPI)
\citep{huttlin2021bioplex,luck2020huri,oughtred2021biogrid}, shared complex membership
\citep{tsitsiridis2023corum}, and functional association \citep{szklarczyk2023string}, the most numerous
and least certain type. Functional associations are $85\%$ of the edges (Appendix~\ref{app:causes}), the
type a skeptic would suspect of adding noise (cause E in \S\ref{sec:causes}).

\paragraph{Model.} EG-IPG (Evidence-Gated Intervention-Informed Protein-Program Graph;
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} in Appendix~\ref{app:arch}) describes each target only by fixed, precomputed features of its protein, never by
a learned label for the gene itself, so it cannot memorize genes and must generalize to unseen ones. Its
encoder passes \emph{messages} over a sampled two-step neighborhood of the target: each protein updates
its \emph{state}, a vector of numbers, from signed signals sent by its graph neighbors, and each edge's
signal is scaled by a learned gate between $0$ and $1$ that depends on the culture condition and the
edge's evidence. A learned mixing weight $\gamma$ combines this graph-based prediction with one that
skips the graph encoder, and $\gamma{=}0$ recovers the no-graph model exactly
(Appendix~\ref{app:arch}).

\paragraph{The four models.} The four models differ only in how the graph enters
(Table~\ref{tab:arms}), so each comparison removes part of one architecture. We call them the
no-graph, typed, gated and untyped models, and the comparisons h1 (gated against no graph), h2a (typed
against no graph), h2b (gated against typed) and the untyped comparison. h1 is
the hypothesis the model was built to test and the subject of our main negative result. The approach can
work only if curated interactions say something about how genes respond together that the target's own
features do not, and if the gate can switch off the edges that say nothing while keeping the rest;
\S\ref{sec:confound} shows a penalty silently switching off every edge.

\section{Results: observed outcome}
\label{sec:outcome}

\subsection{A default penalty switched the graph off}
\label{sec:confound}

Our first five-seed comparison found the graph useless: the graph model could not be told apart from the
no-graph model, and we recorded a clean negative result. A later check of the gates showed why: they sat
near $10^{-7}$, in effect zero, so the graph had been switched off before it learned anything.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{lambda_sweep.pdf}
\caption{The default penalty weight silently disables the graph. \emph{One} seed per run, twenty-two

(Appendix~\ref{app:archsearch} explains why one seed suffices); a gate below $10^{-3}$ counts as dead.
(a) Final mean gate (left axis) and fraction of dead gates (right axis) against the penalty weight
$\lambda_{\mathrm{graph}}$; dotted, the default weight $10^{-2}$; band, the five scored full-data runs at
$\lambda_{\mathrm{graph}}{=}0$ (at the default, full-data gates sat at $\sim$$10^{-7}$). (b) Mean gate
per epoch, one line per penalty weight, dashed at the dead-gate threshold: weights from $10^{-3}$ up are
dead from the second epoch, $10^{-5}$ from the twelfth; none recovers.}
\label{fig:lambda}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Mechanism.} The training loss carries a penalty meant to keep the graph sparse and to
discount low-confidence edges:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{graph}}=\tfrac{1}{B}\sum_{r,b,e}\big(|\alpha^{r}_{b,e}| + (1-\mathrm{conf}^{r}_{b,e})(\alpha^{r}_{b,e})^2\big),
\label{eq:graphloss}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha^{r}_{b,e}$ is the gate on edge $e$ of type $r$ in the neighborhood sampled for training
example $b$, and $\mathrm{conf}^{r}_{b,e}$ is that edge's confidence. The sum runs over \emph{every edge
of every sampled neighborhood}, tens of thousands of them, but is divided only by the number of examples
in a training batch, $B$, never by the number of edges, so its size depends on how many edges the
sampler draws. At the default weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{graph}}{=}10^{-2}$ it is $\approx$$103\times$ the
error on the predicted programs, which is an average rather than a sum. Training changes each number in the model
(each \emph{parameter}) in small steps that lower the loss, and our optimizer, AdamW
\citep{kingma2015adam,loshchilov2019adamw}, divides each step by the typical size of that parameter's
recent gradients, so parameters move at a similar rate however hard the loss pushes; a penalty this large
therefore plausibly sets the \emph{direction} in which the gates move, not only their speed, turning a
mild preference into their main objective.

\paragraph{Signature, and why it is dangerous.} Every gate falls to $\sim$$10^{-7}$ within the first
epoch, in all five seeds; across penalty weights the final gate falls as the weight grows, and no
penalized run recovers (Figure~\ref{fig:lambda}). The run does not crash, warn or diverge, and reports a
plausible \textsc{systema}. With every gate near zero, no messages pass along the edges, so the
comparison no longer tests message passing, and its small difference and narrow interval mean nothing.
Expecting no benefit, we believed it.

\paragraph{Fix and precondition.} The fix that worked is one line: set $\lambda_{\mathrm{graph}}{=}0$.
Dividing the penalty by the number of edges does not rescue the gates, consistent with the direction
argument above: in a pilot run $98\%$ were dead after one epoch. Removing the penalty raised the mean gate
by a factor of $4.5\times10^{6}$, from $\sim$$10^{-7}$ to a live $0.27$ to $0.78$ in the checkpoints we
scored. Yet h1 barely moved, from $-0.0019$ to $-0.0009$ (\S\ref{sec:null}), so the repair did not create
the negative result; that before-and-after comparison is the only use we make of the dead-gate runs' h1. We
now read the mean gate before any result and reject a run below a dead-gate threshold of $10^{-3}$, where
the comparison cannot decide anything. The check takes three minutes; a training run takes hours.

\subsection{With the graph switched on, it still does not help}
\label{sec:null}

In the corrected five-seed comparison with working gates (Table~\ref{tab:family}), the gated model
cannot be told apart from the no-graph model: its interval crosses zero and it passes neither
correction. The typed model, with every edge always on, is worse than no graph on this split, the only
comparison in the family to pass correction. Both conclusions hold under Pearson-delta, the same
correlation without subtracting the average training response, which varies less between seeds and so
has more power (Table~\ref{tab:family}, last column; cause B in Appendix~\ref{app:causes}); under it h2b
and the untyped comparison also pass correction. h1
again passes neither correction on two stricter splits, which use a lower sequence-similarity threshold
and larger groups and so keep more paralogs apart (Figure~\ref{fig:floor}a; Appendix~\ref{app:folds}); on the first it is $+0.0082$, positive in all five
seeds, with uncorrected $p{=}0.025$.

\paragraph{Our first significant result, that the typed model hurts, did not replicate.} The harder split
also tests whether h2a holds up, and it does not (Figure~\ref{fig:floor}a). At the same $n{=}5$ the typed
model's deficit there is $-0.0012$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0074,+0.0050]$, $p=0.61$, clearing neither

estimate holds but its seed noise more than triples (Appendix~\ref{app:folds}), so ``an always-on typed
graph hurts'' holds on one split only.

\subsection{The negative result replicates, but it is about our encoder}
\label{sec:repl}

We repeated the comparisons on seven more Perturb-seq screens from scPerturb \citep{peidli2024scperturb}
(Figure~\ref{fig:repl}), covering four cell types other than T cells, genes switched off as well as
switched on, and a hundredfold range in the number of targets. Six of the seven have a single culture
condition, where the gate cannot vary with condition, so the pre-registration made h2a, not h1, their main
comparison in advance. Each dataset has its own blocked split, its own programs fit on
its training data, four seeds per model and multiple-test correction (Appendix~\ref{app:repl}).

\paragraph{The typed graph shows no benefit.} Pooled over the seven replication datasets, the typed graph
is worth $+0.0018$ \textsc{systema}, with a random-effects $95\%$ CI of $[-0.0028,+0.0065]$ that bounds
any benefit (the fixed-effect interval also crosses zero, and the spread between datasets is not
significant; Appendix~\ref{app:repl}). Frangieh, the only replication dataset with more than one culture condition and so the only one
that can test h1, is underpowered, not evidence of no effect.

\paragraph{The exception points at our encoder.} On Replogle RPE1, in retinal pigment epithelial
cells, the untyped model beats the no-graph model by $+0.0675$ \textsc{systema}, all four seeds
positive ($p{=}0.009$, Bonferroni and Holm both $0.019$ in that dataset's family of two), while the typed
model goes the other way (Figure~\ref{fig:repl}). Where the graph does help, our typed
encoder does not show it.

RPE1 is not the only case. On our own screen the untyped model gains $+0.0043$ \textsc{systema} at
$n{=}7$ (all seven seeds positive, Bonferroni $0.027$ and Holm $0.020$), where the typed model loses
(Figure~\ref{fig:causes}a). Across the eight datasets, however, three pass correction and \emph{they
disagree in sign}: the reference screen and RPE1 gain, while Norman ($-0.0790$), a gene-activation screen,
loses (Figure~\ref{fig:repl}b); all four of its seeds are negative and none was dropped. Neither cell type
nor perturbation direction predicts the sign, and Norman's loss cannot be cleanly separated from its
smaller number of programs. Pooled over the five datasets run with all $K{=}128$ programs, the untyped
comparison is $+0.0141$ ($p{=}0.0056$), but that pooling is post hoc and fails the Hartung-Knapp
adjustment for few datasets ($p{=}0.24$; Appendix~\ref{app:repl}). Read by the untyped model, the same graph moves predictions strongly, better on two
datasets and worse on one, while the typed model passes correction on none of the seven replication
screens. This is the evidence for cause C (\S\ref{sec:causes}), which we had first
recorded as refuted.

\paragraph{C. The architecture is wrong.} We first recorded this cause as refuted, on a $14$-run
architecture search whose single-seed, five-epoch runs could not resolve differences this small
(Appendix~\ref{app:archsearch}). Running all four models at $n{=}7$ on the frozen split locates the
damage instead (Figure~\ref{fig:causes}a): the typed static encoder causes it, and the condition gate
repairs part of it, unreliably (the repair fails correction), rather than adding signal. Most of the
damage is not about evidence types. The typed model adds up each edge type's incoming messages, so a
protein with many partners receives a large sum; scaling each message down by the degrees of the two proteins
its edge joins recovers $79\%$ of the gap between the typed and untyped models (a family of one,
uncorrected $p{=}0.0245$) yet still does not beat no graph, so the deficit looks like an artifact of how
messages are added up (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}). Part of the typed and gated models' cost may also be
that they peak within four epochs and the others only at epochs $13$ to $19$ (Appendix~\ref{app:causes}). \textbf{Survives}; it best explains the negative result.

\paragraph{D. The baseline was never graph-free.} The usual no-graph baseline has message passing off,
but its fixed target features carry graph-derived information: three PPI degree counts (the protein's
numbers of physical, functional and shared-complex partners) and a $128$-d PINNACLE embedding. Setting
those to zero as well lowers \textsc{systema} by $0.0036$, a drop that survives correction
(Figure~\ref{fig:causes}b), while gated message passing over roughly eight
million typed edges (h1), the quantity the paper set out to measure, has no detectable effect. Whether
this per-protein summary of the graph is worth \emph{more} than h1 is not settled: paired by seed it
comes out ahead in all five seeds, but its interval crosses zero. A comparison that calls one model ``no
graph'' while feeding it a summary of the graph does not measure what its name says. \textbf{Survives}
(Appendix~\ref{app:causes}).

\subsection{Boundary conditions}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Bounds and detection.} No split shows a gated-model benefit that survives correction, and
each split's interval bounds the effect only on that split (Figure~\ref{fig:floor}a;
Appendix~\ref{app:folds}). In the planted-signal test, at four seeds the gated model's difference from no
graph passes correction at no injected size up to $0.40$, and the untyped model's from $0.02$, but not for a scrambled graph; as a gain over its own no-injection result, neither
model passes (Figure~\ref{fig:floor}b; Appendix~\ref{app:floor}). Our negative
result thus describes what this encoder can resolve with this many seeds, not whether a
protein-interaction prior helps.

\paragraph{Scope.} Our pre-registered rule for building responses first excluded four of six candidates by a
standard the reference data do not meet (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}); even after its
amendment, h1 rests on one replication dataset and none shares the reference \emph{cell type}
(Appendix~\ref{app:repl}). Gains elsewhere \citep{wenkel2026txpert,cole2026foundation} use other
splits and scores, outside our bound. Scored their way, the gated
model shows no whole-profile benefit; the untyped comparison \emph{reverses sign} between TxPert's
and \textsc{gears}' scores, and by gene rank the gated model passes correction for the graph on the
least-changed genes and, under \textsc{gears}' score, against it on the top twenty
(Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}).

\section{Conclusion}

In testing whether a protein-interaction graph helps predict T-cell responses to a knocked-down gene,
the harder problem was trusting our measurement: a standard penalty had switched off the mechanism
under test. With the gates repaired the gated model cannot be told apart from no graph, the typed graph's
lack of benefit holds on seven more screens, and the only pre-registered comparison to pass correction at
five seeds failed to replicate. The untyped model, run as a diagnostic, clears correction on three datasets in
disagreeing directions, a sign that neither cell type nor perturbation direction predicts and
that we cannot cleanly separate from the number of programs, so our negative result is evidence about the
encoder, not against the prior. A graph ablation should also strip the ``no graph'' baseline's
graph-derived features, and a negative result about a prior should test its simplest encoder. For us that
was one extra model; without it we would have published the wrong cause and, had we stopped at the
first five datasets, all favoring the untyped model, a sign the sixth reversed.

\section{Model architecture}
\label{app:arch}

EG-IPG has four modules (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}): an encoder for the perturbation and its context; the typed, gated graph encoder; a program decoder with a mixing weight between graph-based and expression-only predictions; and a sparse rationale head in the GNNExplainer lineage \citep{ying2019gnnexplainer}, which picks out the few edges behind each prediction and is fitted after the predictor is frozen.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{eg_ipg_architecture_compact.pdf}
\caption{EG-IPG, and where it breaks. Modules M1 to M3 form the predictor that every comparison
scores; the sparse rationale head M4, which picks out the few edges behind each prediction, is fitted
only after the predictor is frozen. The boxed sparsity penalty (Eq.~\ref{eq:graphloss}) acts on the
learnable edge gates, the part that separates the gated model from the typed model, whose gates are
fixed at $1$, and it is what switched the graph off (\S\ref{sec:confound}). The paragraphs
below explain every label.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Reading Figure~\ref{fig:arch}.} Inputs (left): the target gene $g$, the culture condition $c$, the donor $d$, entered through that donor's control-cell profile, the covariates $q_{\mathrm{pre}}$ known before any response is seen (the number of guide RNAs and a single-guide estimate), the gene's baseline expression and the fixed protein features below. M1 combines them into $h_{\mathrm{do}}$. M2, the graph encoder, reads the target's sampled neighborhood $\mathcal{N}_k(g)$ and the condition embedding $h_{\mathrm{cond}}(c)$, which feeds the gates, and outputs $h_{\mathrm{graph}}$. M3 mixes the graph-based and no-graph predictions with the learned weight $\gamma$, labeled a mixture gate in the figure but not an edge gate. The outputs are the predicted change in programs, $\Delta\hat z$, and in genes, $\Delta\hat x$, and a predicted uncertainty $\hat U$ that this paper does not use. Training has two stages. Stage A trains M1 to M3 on a loss that sums, each with its weight $\lambda$: $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{resp}}$, the error on the program changes; $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gene}}$, the error on the gene changes; $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DE}}$, a classification loss for calling each gene up or down; $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inv}}$, a penalty that keeps the program prediction the same whichever donor's control cells are used; and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{graph}}$, the edge penalty of Eq.~\ref{eq:graphloss}, boxed because it is the confound of \S\ref{sec:confound}. Stage B, with the predictor frozen, fits the uncertainty and the rationale head M4, which scores each edge as its gate times a learned edge score, $\mathrm{imp}_e=\alpha_e\,\sigma(\mathrm{score}(e))$, and keeps the fifteen highest-scoring edges as the rationale $\hat S$.

\paragraph{Perturbation and context encoder.} The target protein is described by fixed features only, with \emph{no} learned gene-identity embedding, so the model cannot memorize which target is which and must generalize to unseen genes. The features are an ESM-2 650M protein-sequence embedding ($1280$-d) \citep{lin2023esm2}, a PINNACLE embedding ($128$-d) \citep{li2024pinnacle}, three PPI degree counts and the gene's baseline expression in control cells, $1412$-d in total. These are combined with an embedding of the culture condition and with donor context into one intervention vector $h_{\mathrm{do}}\in\mathbb{R}^{256}$. This vector, and therefore the no-graph model, contains graph-derived features (\S\ref{sec:causes}, cause D). How often each feature is actually present over the $33{,}983$ DE rows: ESM-2 resolves for $99.4\%$, physical and functional PPI degree are nonzero for $96.5\%$ and $98.4\%$, complex degree for $29.5\%$, and PINNACLE for $9.2\%$ (the embedding store returns a zero vector when a protein is missing).

\paragraph{Typed, gated graph encoder.} Around each target we sample a two-step neighborhood of at most $512$ nodes and run $L{=}3$ relational message-passing layers \citep{schlichtkrull2018rgcn}, which keep separate weights for each edge type. For an edge $u\!\to\!v$ of type $r$ the message is
\begin{equation}
m_{u\to v}^{(l),r}=\tanh\!\big(W^{(l),r}_{\mathrm{sign}}h_u^{(l-1)}\big)\odot\mathrm{ReLU}\!\big(W^{(l),r}_{\mathrm{mag}}h_u^{(l-1)}\big),
\end{equation}
a message that can be positive or negative, as activation or inhibition would be ($\tanh$ factor), times a non-negative strength ($\mathrm{ReLU}$ factor). The condition gate
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{u\to v}^{r}(c)=\sigma\!\big(w_{\mathrm{gate}}^{r}\cdot[\,h_{\mathrm{cond}}(c)\,\|\,f_e\,]+b_{\mathrm{gate}}^{r}\big)\in[0,1]
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
multiplies each message before a node's incoming messages are combined, so the same physical edge can be on in one condition and off in another. Unlike graph attention, it is an independent sigmoid for each edge, computed from the culture condition $c$ and the edge's evidence features $f_e$, not a similarity score normalized over a neighborhood. Each node adds its new information to its old state (a residual update), and a $4$-head cross-attention readout, which weights each node by its relevance to the target's own features $h_{\mathrm{do}}$, pools the node states into $h_{\mathrm{graph}}\in\mathbb{R}^{256}$.

\paragraph{The other encoders, and PINNACLE.} The untyped model replaces these layers with a graph convolutional network (GCN) \citep{kipf2017gcn}, which treats all edges alike and has no gates, and our architecture search (Appendix~\ref{app:archsearch}) also tried graph attention \citep{brody2022gatv2}. PINNACLE \citep{li2024pinnacle} compresses a cell-type-specific interaction network into one vector per protein, which carries graph information into a model without message passing, the no-graph model included (cause D in \S\ref{sec:causes}); here it has no measurable effect (Appendix~\ref{app:ablate}).

\paragraph{Decoder and mixing weight.} The decoder predicts a change in the programs from the graph, and in parallel an expression-only prediction that bypasses the graph, then mixes the two with a learned weight $\gamma$. Switching the graph encoder off forces $\gamma{=}0$ and recovers the no-graph model exactly, so the four models are nested ablations of one architecture rather than comparisons between separate code paths.

\paragraph{The search could not have answered its question.} Four of its runs drop the typing and gating (three appear as the untyped rows of Table~\ref{tab:archsearch}), so in principle it could have found cause C, but it was underpowered. Every run used a single seed, and our protocol gives no $p$-value at $n{=}1$, because one seed is not a paired result; no run was decidable on its own, and the search as a whole distinguished nothing. Across all $14$ runs the best is $+0.0051$ against the no-graph baseline and the worst is $-0.0037$, a spread of $0.0089$. If all $14$ designs were equally good and only the training seed varied (the \emph{seed noise alone}, $\mathrm{sd}=0.0043$ on the frozen split), their scores would be expected to span $0.0147$. The whole search is narrower than what re-running one architecture with new seeds produces, and its best run would need $12$ paired seeds to pass our correction rule with $80\%$ power. Its budget was also biased. Every run trained for \emph{five} epochs. In our full-length runs the typed and gated models reach their best validation loss by epoch $3$ and the untyped and no-graph models not until epochs $13$ to $19$ (Appendix~\ref{app:causes}). If the inner split trains the same way, five epochs catch the typed and gated runs at their best and the rest well short of theirs. That budget favors the runs that came out on top, and nothing here separates how fast a model converges from how good it ends up. We ranked $14$ architectures on differences we could not resolve, at a budget unlike the one we report, and then read the ranking as a refutation.

\paragraph{Why Figure~\ref{fig:lambda} may rest on one seed when this table may not.} The penalty sweep behind Figure~\ref{fig:lambda} is also one run per point, so the objection above applies to it on its face. We accept it there for a reason that does not carry over. That figure measures a gate collapsing across seven orders of magnitude, from $0.76$ to $\sim$$10^{-7}$, and the largest seed-to-seed spread we measure anywhere in this paper is a factor of two on a quantity of order $10^{-2}$. The architecture search, by contrast, spans differences of $0.0089$ against a seed spread of $0.0043$, where one seed decides the ranking. The sweep clears its own noise by a margin that new seeds cannot close; the search does not.

\section{Which part of the typed design does the damage?}
\label{app:mechanism}

The typed model's deficit comes mostly from summing each edge type's messages without normalizing them, not from the edge types themselves and not from the model's size. \S\ref{sec:causes} traces the deficit to the typed encoder: $-0.0120$ \textsc{systema} at $n{=}7$, seven of seven seeds, surviving both corrections. Two explanations are tangled inside that number. Splitting edges by type may be the wrong design; or typed message passing simply has four times the message parameters over the same edges, in which case the damage is about capacity and has nothing to do with evidence types. Two extra models separate them (Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}), each registered with its decision rule before either was trained.

\textsc{typed\_shared} uses one message module for all four relations (the three evidence types, plus the links from proteins to the complex nodes they belong to) instead of one each, cutting $2{,}396{,}160$ message parameters to $599{,}040$ and the model's total parameters by $34\%$ ($5{,}254{,}884$ to $3{,}457{,}764$), and changes nothing else. \textsc{typed\_permuted} keeps one module per relation but gives every protein-protein edge a randomly reassigned relation label, keeping each relation's edge count exactly. The relabeling happens \emph{after} the neighborhood is sampled, because the sampler ranks candidate neighbors by relation: permuting the stored graph would have changed which neighbors enter the subgraph, not only which weights process them.

Neither explains the deficit. Cutting the message parameters fourfold, a third of the whole model, moves \textsc{systema} by $+0.0004$; if the deficit were about capacity or overfitting, that change would have fixed it. Replacing the true relation labels with random ones of identical sizes moves it by $+0.0065$, which does not pass correction. That estimate is \emph{positive}, so if anything the curated labels are slightly worse than random ones, but under our rule it is a null and we report it as one.

What remains is what all three typed models (the typed model and its two variants) share and the untyped one does not. Both diagnostic models sit well below the untyped \textsc{gcn} ($-0.0172$ and $-0.0111$) and below the no-graph baseline ($-0.0126$ and $-0.0065$), so the deficit survives both changes. The remaining candidates concern the form of the message rather than where it goes: signed messages, the per-edge feature term, the node type for complex membership, the residual feed-forward block, and summing an edge type's messages instead of normalizing them by degree.

We had reported the last of these as refuted. The architecture search that refuted it spans $0.0089$ across all $14$ of its runs, while the untyped-minus-typed gap we measure at $n{=}5$ on the frozen split is $0.0176$. A search whose whole range is half the difference under study was not resolving these models, so ``refuted within the searched space'' was never established for normalization either.

\paragraph{Degree normalization recovers most of the deficit.} \textsc{typed\_gcnnorm} is the typed model with each relation's messages weighted by $1/\sqrt{d_i d_j}$, where $d_i$ and $d_j$ are the degrees of the edge's two ends, instead of summed. Nothing else changes, not even the parameter count, since the shared-module model had already excluded capacity. Against the typed runs at the same five seeds it recovers $+0.0139$ \textsc{systema} ($95\%$ CI $[+0.0029,+0.0248]$, five of five seeds positive), or $79\%$ of the $+0.0176$ gap to the untyped model. Most of the deficit is therefore the unnormalized sum per relation, not the relation labels: a relation's contribution grows with its degree, so \textsc{functional\_assoc} ($85\%$ of all edges, median score $0.209$) dominates each node's update by sheer count.

\paragraph{It is not a graph benefit.} First, repairing the encoder buys nothing against no graph: \textsc{typed\_gcnnorm} is $+0.0008$ against the no-graph baseline ($[-0.0047,+0.0062]$, $p=0.71$, three of five seeds) and cannot be told apart from the untyped model ($-0.0037$, $p=0.22$). Fixing the aggregation removes the damage and leaves the plain negative result of \S\ref{sec:null}. Second, this is a family of \emph{one}, where Bonferroni and Holm change nothing, so ``passes both corrections'' means only the uncorrected $p=0.0245$; the three other registered message-form models would make a family of four ($m{=}4$), where it would not pass. What does not depend on the correction is that all five seeds agree in sign and that the recovered share is large.

\paragraph{Why we stopped at one model.} The remainder that the other three would divide up is $\textsc{untyped\_gnn}-\textsc{typed\_gcnnorm}=+0.0037$, against a minimum detectable effect of $0.0096$ at five seeds, $2.6\times$ the whole remainder and larger still against any one model's share. Detecting the remainder needs about $21$ seeds, and half of it about $77$, so three more models at five seeds would have been underpowered by construction. Stopping also keeps the family at one and so keeps the $p$ above, but the power calculation, not the $p$, is why we stopped.

\section{The replication datasets and protocol}
\label{app:repl}

Seven public screens let us repeat the comparison, but none is a T-cell screen and most have far fewer targets than the reference screen, so each is a weaker test than the reference. We pre-registered the multi-dataset replication before running it: the family of comparisons and its correction, the number of seeds, the primary comparison for each dataset, the criteria for stopping and the rules for combining results. The survey of candidate datasets that went with it bears on how any such replication should be read.

First, few candidates come close to the reference screen's number of targets. The reference screen has $11{,}526$ gene targets. One harmonized public alternative \citep{peidli2024scperturb} is comparable, the Replogle K562 genome-wide screen at $9{,}730$ targets with a response profile, and it gives the tightest interval of the seven (\S\ref{sec:repl}). The rest are far smaller: the largest is Replogle RPE1 at $2{,}122$, the largest with more than one experimental condition is Frangieh melanoma at $238$ over three conditions, and the two T-cell candidates, Shifrut in primary cells and Datlinger in the Jurkat cell line, yield $20$ and $30$. Because the split holds out whole groups of similar sequences, the number of targets sets how many groups there are to hold out, so outside the genome-wide screen a replication is a weaker measurement than the reference.

Second, the number of targets and the number of conditions are anti-correlated across candidates. Every dataset with enough targets for a well-powered split has a single condition, where the gate cannot vary with condition, so h1 cannot test condition-dependent gating there and the pre-registration treats it as undefined. The primary comparison on those datasets must be h2a, and the pre-registration assigns it per dataset in advance, not after seeing results.

\paragraph{What we built and trained.} Ten candidates were built under the corrected DE rule, eight passed an on-target quality check (QC), and seven were trained to completion: Frangieh melanoma, Replogle RPE1, Replogle K562-essential, Replogle K562 genome-wide, Norman K562, and Tian iPSC-neuron CRISPRi and CRISPRa (Table~\ref{tab:repl}). The two that failed QC, Datlinger and Shifrut, were the pool's only T-cell data, which is why the replication covers four cell types but not the reference's own. The number of programs does not carry over: $K$ cannot exceed the number of training rows, and a replication dataset has one row per (target, condition), so the reference $K{=}128$ is not possible everywhere. Tian CRISPRi runs at $K{=}32$, and Tian CRISPRa and Norman at $K{=}16$, each a labeled deviation fixed before the first run (Table~\ref{tab:repl}).

\paragraph{The $K{=}128$ subset disagrees with the full pool.} Amendment 3.1 of the pre-registration asks for two pooled estimates: one over all datasets, and one over only the datasets that run with the reference model's $K{=}128$ programs (\S\ref{sec:repl}). It also fixed in advance that where the two disagree, the $K{=}128$ subset is the one that describes the reference model. We report both and settle neither, because the pre-registration pools only h1 and h2a, so for the untyped model both estimates are post hoc. Over all eight datasets, random effects give $+0.0091$ ($p{=}0.12$) at $I^2{=}87.5\%$, with CI $[-0.0024,+0.0207]$; random effects let the true effect differ between datasets, the right choice when datasets disagree this much. Over the five $K{=}128$ datasets it is $+0.0141$ ($[+0.0041,+0.0240]$, $p{=}0.0056$), and all five are positive. The datasets still disagree strongly within that subset ($I^2{=}87.7\%$). The only negative dataset, Norman, is one of the three that ran below $K{=}128$, so its opposite sign cannot be cleanly separated from its eightfold cut in the number of programs. The cut does not explain the sign either: the other two datasets below $K{=}128$ carry the second and third largest positive effects (Tian CRISPRi $+0.0281$, CRISPRa $+0.0226$), and one negative among three against none among five arises easily by chance ($p{=}0.375$, Fisher exact). Neither estimate is robust to the Hartung-Knapp adjustment, which corrects a pooled interval for having only a few datasets, usually widening it. The pre-registration did not specify it, so we report it only as a sensitivity check: it widens the five-dataset interval to $[-0.0139,+0.0421]$ ($p{=}0.24$), the eight-dataset one to $[-0.0201,+0.0384]$ ($p{=}0.48$), and the typed model's seven-screen random-effects bound of \S\ref{sec:repl} to $[-0.0053,+0.0089]$.

\paragraph{On-target check.} The code that converts each public dataset into our format can produce a matrix of the right shape with wrong values, so we checked the one trained multi-condition dataset against biology it cannot fake: in Frangieh, knocking out a gene lowers that gene's own expression (own-gene $\log_2$ fold change mean $-0.613$, negative for $90\%$ of targeted genes, against $-0.002$ and $63\%$ for random genes).

\section{Splits, re-drawn splits, and what each split can bound}
\label{app:folds}

Each split bounds the effect only on itself. Re-drawing the same split specification moves results as much as changing the specification does, and the training seed is the largest source of noise.

\paragraph{How well the frozen split is blocked.} Blocking by sequence group reduces the paralogs shared by training and validation; it does not remove them. The split's own audit compares only the training set with the sequestered split, a test set held back and never scored. Run between training and validation targets, the same function finds $24.0\%$ of validation targets ($356$ of $1{,}482$) with a training target at centered cosine $\geq 0.85$ (maximum $0.996$), against $60\%$ for a naive split that deals out every twentieth gene. Some above-threshold pairs land in different groups because the clustering does not chain similar pairs together, which is what keeps the groups from merging into one giant group.

\paragraph{A harder split.} Lowering the sequence-similarity threshold from $0.85$ to $0.75$ and raising the group-size cap from $5\%$ to $15\%$ blocks more paralogs and lowers the median train-to-sequestered-split sequence cosine from $0.796$ to $0.741$. We compare only paired comparisons within each split: the validation sets differ in size and composition, so absolute \textsc{systema} is not comparable between splits even when a paired comparison is, and the re-draws below show why we do not treat that cosine as a clean measure of difficulty either. On this split nothing in the family passes correction: h2b and the untyped comparison shrink as h2a does ($+0.0122\!\to\!+0.0017$, $+0.0045\!\to\!+0.0028$). The gated graph stays tied with no graph ($\Delta\,\textsc{systema}=+0.0005$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0105,+0.0115]$, $p=0.90$, $n{=}5$ paired seeds, per-seed differences $-0.0003$, $+0.0035$, $+0.0032$, $-0.0138$, $+0.0100$). The point estimate here is nominally \emph{positive}, unlike the frozen split's $-0.0009$, and we read nothing into that: the interval straddles zero almost symmetrically, the comparison passes neither correction, and one seed ($-0.0138$) shifts the mean by more than the mean's own size. The sign is undetermined at this budget, and the harder split gives a wider interval, not a tighter bound.

\paragraph{A third split.} Two splits invite a story about difficulty, so we ran a third at an intermediate threshold (Table~\ref{tab:folds}). The corrected h1 verdict is the same on all three, indistinguishable from no graph, but the point estimates are not: $-0.0009$, $+0.0082$, $+0.0005$. On the intermediate split all five seeds favor the graph and the \emph{uncorrected} interval excludes zero ($p=0.025$); it fails the correction for multiple tests ($p_{\mathrm{Bonf}}=0.101$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.101$), so the verdict stands, but reporting only the two splits whose estimates sit near zero would be selective. h2a failed to replicate twice, for different reasons. On the intermediate split h2a is $-0.0122$, within $7\%$ of the frozen $-0.0131$, yet passes no correction ($p=0.23$) because the typed model's across-seed standard deviation is $0.0169$ there against $0.0049$ on the frozen split. On the harder split the estimate itself collapses to $-0.0012$ ($p=0.61$). The per-seed differences show why: $-0.0010$, $-0.0013$, $-0.0033$, $-0.0070$, $+0.0066$. One seed reverses sign. Only the second failure, the collapse on the harder split, says anything about the effect.

\paragraph{One split specification, three re-draws.} Three splits are not three levels of difficulty. Holding the threshold and the cap fixed at the intermediate setting and changing only the split seed, the random number that decides which group goes where, three draws of the \emph{same} split specification give median train-to-sequestered-split cosines of $0.759$, $0.793$ and $0.862$. Partition noise alone spreads the statistic over $0.103$, against $0.056$ for the entire designed range across all three thresholds ($0.796$ to $0.741$): re-drawing one split moves the difficulty statistic almost twice as far as deliberately changing the threshold does, and the third re-draw is \emph{easier} than the frozen split it was meant to sit below. The draws also differ in structure, sharing only $20\%$ of their validation targets and differing twofold in validation size, and the no-graph baseline moves with them: at five paired seeds per re-draw it scores $0.0991$, $0.0942$ and $0.0845$ \textsc{systema}, a $0.0146$ spread on our most stable model, larger than any effect in Table~\ref{tab:folds}. It does not track the cosine statistic, since the nominally hardest re-draw scores highest. It orders with validation-set size, which the re-draws moved from $3{,}632$ to $7{,}216$ without our asking, though with three re-draws that ordering would arise by chance one time in six, so we offer it as the more plausible reading rather than a measured relationship.

The comparison moves too. Running the graph-versus-no-graph comparison on all three re-draws at five paired seeds each (the first re-draw is the intermediate split of Table~\ref{tab:folds}) gives h1 $=+0.0082$ (uncorrected $95\%$ CI $[+0.0017,+0.0148]$, $p=0.025$), $+0.0026$ ($[+0.0005,+0.0047]$, $p=0.027$) and $+0.0021$ ($[-0.0024,+0.0065]$, $p=0.266$). The experiment, the specification and the five seeds are the same and only the partition draw differs, yet the estimate spans fourfold and \emph{the qualitative verdict flips}. Two re-draws give an interval excluding zero at $p\approx0.03$ and the third does not, so an author who drew the first would report an apparent graph benefit and one who drew the third would report nothing. All three estimates are positive, and none passes Bonferroni over the pre-registered family of four ($0.101$, $0.109$, $1.000$). That is why the corrected verdict carries the paper and the uncorrected intervals serve as a cautionary example.

Each comparison is valid within its own split, but the \emph{ordering} in Table~\ref{tab:folds} carries far less information than three rows suggest. We therefore do not claim that the effect grows or shrinks with how unlike the training genes the validation genes are, and we suggest the same caution for any split sweep that uses one draw per setting: a threshold that appears to control difficulty may move it less than the partition seed does. Establishing a difficulty effect needs several draws per level, so that differences between levels can be read against the spread within a level.

\paragraph{The nested variance decomposition.} We then ran that design. A \emph{level} is one threshold and cap setting, and a \emph{cell} is one draw of it, trained with five paired seeds (seven on the frozen split). For h2a we re-drew the harder split ($0.75/0.15$) three times and added a fourth level at $0.70/0.05$, giving six cells over four levels; for h1 we use the three intermediate re-draws above. Together they split the variance three ways: between levels, between re-draws within a level, and between seeds within a re-draw. For h2a the components are $\mathrm{sd}_{\text{seed}}=0.0107$ ($26$ df), $\mathrm{sd}_{\text{level}}=0$ ($3$ df) and $\mathrm{sd}_{\text{redraw}}=0.0003$ ($2$ df), where df, the degrees of freedom, counts the independent comparisons behind each estimate. For h1, over five cells at three levels, they are $\mathrm{sd}_{\text{seed}}=0.0057$ ($22$ df), $\mathrm{sd}_{\text{level}}=0.0027$ ($2$ df) and $\mathrm{sd}_{\text{redraw}}=0.0023$ ($2$ df). These figures were corrected after review (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

Partition noise looks \emph{comparison-dependent}: h1's re-draw component is $0.0023$ against h2a's $0.0003$, roughly sevenfold, as the flipping verdicts above suggest. The two are not measured on the same partitions, though. h1's three re-draws sit at the intermediate $0.80/0.10$ setting and h2a's at the harder $0.75/0.15$, because those are the levels at which each comparison's models were replicated, so the sevenfold difference is confounded with difficulty level: it may be the comparison, or it may be the level. h2a shows no difficulty effect at all: once seed and re-draw noise are subtracted, its level component is zero, so its four level means differ by no more than that noise predicts. The term that dominates both comparisons is the seed. For h2a the training seed is the only component distinguishable from zero, and the only one here with enough degrees of freedom to be estimated precisely; for h1 the seed leads the difficulty setting by $2.1\times$, with $2$ df behind the level term. So a split sweep with one seed per split mostly measures seed noise.

\paragraph{Per-split upper limits.} The quantity that matters is each split's interval, and it bounds that split only. The per-split upper limits are $+0.0054$ (frozen), $+0.0148$ (intermediate) and $+0.0115$ (harder), so across splits the data allow roughly three times the frozen split's ceiling. No split, and no re-draw of a split, yields a gated-graph benefit that survives correction over the pre-registered family of four, and no single split's upper limit bounds the effect elsewhere.

A feature that the model's inputs list but the data mostly lack changes what an ablation means, in either direction.

\section{The candidate causes in full}
\label{app:causes}

\paragraph{A. The task is close to null for every model.} Centroid accuracy, the fraction of held-out profiles whose prediction is closer to their own observed profile than to any other, sits near its floor ($\approx$$0.001$) for every model, and all four trained models occupy a band from $0.072$ to $0.090$. An independent tabular benchmark reports this exact screen as a near-null setting in which models barely separate from the average perturbed response \citep{royer2026tfm}. If there is little target-specific structure to recover, no prior can route it. That does not excuse the graph, but it bounds how much any component could have contributed, and it is why we report a \emph{bounded} null rather than a claim of zero.

\paragraph{B. The score is not hiding a gated-graph benefit.} \textsc{systema} deliberately withholds credit for predicting the average response, so it could also suppress a real gain. Scored instead with Pearson-delta on the programs, which does not subtract the training mean, h1 stays null ($p=0.64$ for the gated graph; Table~\ref{tab:family}, last column), and program cosine similarity agrees ($-0.0005$, $p=0.65$). Under Pearson-delta the paired per-seed differences have less than half the standard deviation they have under \textsc{systema}, so it has \emph{more} power, yet while it finds nothing for the gated graph, the untyped comparison and the gate's improvement over the typed model pass correction under it. So the score can hide a benefit, but not the one the headline is about. Ruled out for h1; Appendix~\ref{app:metrics} shows how far the other comparisons move when the score changes.

\paragraph{E. The edges we would have pruned move the prediction most.} Functional associations are $85\%$ of the edges and the low-confidence type. Pruning them, the obvious cleanup, lowered \textsc{systema} and the gates in the architecture search, but no single-seed run there is decidable (Appendix~\ref{app:archsearch}). In the rationale audit with live gates, removing every functional-association edge moves the full prediction furthest ($\Delta=0.365$, the distance between the predictions with and without them, against $\leq0.0021$ for BioPlex, HuRI and CORUM). If most edges are noise and what signal there is sits among the least confident ones, an encoder that sums every edge without normalization pays for that noise (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}). Ours can switch edges off, which may be why it ties with no graph instead of showing the typed model's deficit, but gating recovers only enough to break even.

\paragraph{The audit's ratios, at their actual sizes.} Every number in that paragraph is measured against a matched control, which is the right design but produces a ratio, and in a near-null setting a ratio can be large while the quantity it divides is negligible. Three caveats follow, each weakening our earlier reading. First, STRING is $85.4\%$ of the graph's $8{,}029{,}296$ protein-protein edges, against $1.5\%$ for BioPlex, $1.2\%$ for CORUM and $0.6\%$ for HuRI; the remaining $11.3\%$ is BioGRID, which the audit did not remove. Removing STRING therefore removes most of the graph. Per $1\%$ of edges removed it moves the prediction by $0.0043$, against HuRI's $0.0025$ and BioPlex's $0.0014$, a lead of $1.7$ to $3\times$ rather than the two orders of magnitude the raw differences suggest; only CORUM stays far behind, at $0.00002$. Second, on \emph{sufficiency}, how far the prediction from the rationale's edges alone sits from the full prediction (smaller is better), the rationales do \emph{not} beat matched random rationales of the same size. Over $50$ audited predictions, the rationale's mean distance, $1.47$, is larger than the random rationales' by $+0.011$, or $0.8\%$ ($95\%$ CI $[+0.006,+0.016]$, $n{=}50$, $p{=}2.6\times10^{-5}$); it beats random in $4\%$ of cases. Third, on necessity, how much deleting the selected edges changes the prediction, the rationales beat their control by $49\%$, but both quantities are of order $10^{-7}$, so the audit's summary that ``$92\%$ of cases beat random on necessity'' is true and nearly empty: deleting the selected edges moves the prediction by about one part in ten million. The one comparison with a substantive gap is GInX \citep{amara2023ginx}, $0.084$ against random's $0.030$ at $20\%$ sparsity. It keeps only the top-ranked $20\%$ of edges, or a random $20\%$, and measures the same sufficiency distance, so a larger value is worse and it points the same way: the rationale is the less sufficient of the two. Cause E therefore stays at \emph{partly}: removing that edge type moves the prediction most largely because it holds most of the edges, and the rationale itself is no more sufficient than a random one of the same size.

\paragraph{C: the intervals behind the ordering of the models.} Ordered by \textsc{systema}: untyped $0.0904$, no graph $0.0861$, gated $0.0825$, typed $0.0740$. At $n{=}7$ on the frozen split, the typed static encoder costs $-0.0120$ \textsc{systema} ($95\%$ CI $[-0.0160,-0.0080]$, Bonferroni and Holm both $0.001$); the condition gate returns $+0.0085$, whose raw interval excludes zero but which does not survive correction (Bonferroni $0.186$), leaving the gated model at $-0.0036$ against no graph ($95\%$ CI $[-0.0093,+0.0022]$); and the untyped model is $+0.0043$ ($95\%$ CI $[+0.0017,+0.0069]$, paired $p=0.0068$).

\paragraph{C: when each model is scored.} Every model shares the same early-stopping rule on validation loss (patience $10$ epochs without improvement, cap $20$ epochs), and the rule ends them at very different epochs. On the five-seed frozen split the no-graph and untyped runs go to the cap, with best checkpoints at epochs $14$ to $19$ and $13$ to $19$; the typed runs stop after $11$ to $13$ epochs with the checkpoint from epoch $0$ in four of five seeds, and the gated runs with live gates after $11$ to $14$ with checkpoints from epochs $0$ to $3$. The typed and gated models are therefore scored as models that peaked, by validation loss, within their first four epochs. The rule is the same for every model, so this is not a difference in budget, but we did not test whether a longer schedule, or selecting on the endpoint rather than the loss, would change h2a or h1.

\paragraph{D in full: what the ``no graph'' baseline contains.} The fixed feature vector of that model is graph-derived in two places: three PPI degree counts, whose physical and functional components are nonzero for $96.5\%$ and $98.4\%$ of rows (medians $53$ and $604$), and a $128$-d PINNACLE embedding \citep{li2024pinnacle} learned on a cell-type-specific interaction network. The comparison we and others label ``graph versus no graph'' therefore measures \emph{the extra value of message passing over a per-protein summary of the graph}, and the usual ablation cannot see that summary by construction.

A complete version of the ablation is three-way, also stripping graph-derived node features, and we ran it (Appendix~\ref{app:ablate}): zeroing them, with input width and parameter count unchanged, costs $-0.0036$ \textsc{systema} over five paired seeds under family-wise correction ($p=0.0005$). Nearly all of it comes from the three degree counts. Zeroing PINNACLE alone moves \textsc{systema} by $+0.000006$, far less even than its $9.2\%$ coverage would predict: nine percent of the degree counts' effect would be of order $10^{-4}$, and this is fifty times smaller. Thin coverage is part of it; the rest is that the embedding carries almost nothing usable on the rows where it does resolve.

Three degree counts carry a measured effect; the gated encoder (h1), which passes messages over the network they summarize, carries none we can detect. Whether the summary is worth \emph{more} is not settled: paired by seed, the graph-derived features' value minus h1 is $+0.0045$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.0012,+0.0101]$, $p{=}0.093$; a post hoc test), positive in all five seeds. We do not claim that degree is a good predictor in absolute terms; $0.0036$ against a baseline of $0.0857$ is about four percent, and it passes correction largely because the no-graph model is our least noisy one. The claim is about experimental design: the model called ``no graph'' carries a measured graph effect, so a graph ablation should strip it too.

\section{When a pre-registration freezes a mistake}
\label{app:prereg}

Our pre-registration contained a rule that the reference data themselves would have failed, and it nearly cost us the replication.

\paragraph{The rule, and what it cost.} Under the original rule, before the amendment this section is about, we built DE matrices for six harmonized public candidates \citep{peidli2024scperturb}; the corrected rule later built ten (Table~\ref{tab:repl}). The pre-registration fixed the DE unit as ``two or more replicate pseudobulks per (target, condition)'', where a pseudobulk is the summed expression of a group of cells and a replicate is an independent group such as a donor, a batch or a guide RNA, depending on the dataset; each pseudobulk needed at least $25$ cells, and a dataset unable to meet the rule was to be dropped rather than switched to a different test. Four candidates fell out. The clause did what such clauses are for: when Replogle RPE1, the best-powered of those six candidates, yielded only $12$ of $2{,}393$ targets, we did not rescue it by splitting cells into random pseudo-replicates, which would have manufactured precision we did not have.

\paragraph{The audit, and why the rule was wrong.} The clause was also wrong, and it took an audit rather than a result to see it. The response profiles of the reference screen, which we train on, are not built that way. It is a file published with the source screen, computed by its authors over all cells for each (perturbation, condition) against matched controls, with no per-replicate requirement. We had imposed on every candidate replication a standard we never imposed on the data the study rests on, and so discarded the best-powered replication then available for failing a test the reference itself would have failed. Under the reference's own standard, keeping the $25$-cell floor, the same file yields $2{,}122$ targets rather than $12$.

\paragraph{The failure mode this illustrates.} Discussion of pre-registration in machine learning is almost entirely about the failure it prevents: analysis choices made after seeing the results. Pre-registration also has a failure of its own. It fixes the plan when the data are least understood, so a rule written then can be wrong in a way that stays invisible until something forces an audit. The commitment that gives pre-registration its value also makes such an error durable, because relaxing a pre-registered rule looks like the flexibility pre-registration exists to prevent. Our defense is procedural: the amendment is dated, it states that no replication model had been trained when it was written, and it is outcome-symmetric, admitting datasets that could support a positive replication as readily as a null. Any pre-registration in this setting should include an explicit audit step that checks each rule against how the reference data were themselves built, before the rule is frozen.

\section{How large an effect we could have detected}
\label{app:power}

Measured noise sets how small an effect this design could detect, and the answer depends on what a claim must survive: new seeds, a re-drawn split, or new datasets. \paragraph{What we could have detected, simulated rather than approximated.} Power is the chance of detecting a real effect of a given size. We first reported a minimum detectable effect from a normal approximation on five paired per-seed differences, which assumed that the variation between seeds was the only variation. That assumption is no longer needed, because the variance is measured at three levels (Appendix~\ref{app:folds}), and the bar a result must clear here is not $p<0.05$ but a paired $t$-test followed by Bonferroni and Holm over a family of four, which has no closed form. So we simulate it: each replicate draws the whole family, one comparison at the effect under test and three true nulls, runs the pipeline's own test and correction, and we repeat $2{,}000$ times per point. Table~\ref{tab:floor} collects the result. Two calibrations check that the simulation measures what we think. With no true effect, the comparison passes correction at a rate of $0.013$: the corrected rate, not the nominal $0.05$. And the reference screen's corrected-significant graph benefit, the untyped model's $+0.0043$, needs $9$ seeds for $80\%$ power at its own measured spread of $0.0028$; we ran $7$, which the simulation puts at $70\%$ power, and it did survive both corrections.

Our headline null is an estimate of $-0.0009$, an order of magnitude inside the first of those. Appendix~\ref{app:floor} measures the setup directly: the untyped model passes at $0.02$ response SDs, and the gated model passes at no injected size up to $0.40$.

\paragraph{Graph models vary more across seeds.} The no-graph model is stable across seeds and every graph model is not. For the gated model the spread rises with no detectable change in the mean. Message passing over a mostly noisy neighborhood plausibly gives the optimizer a high-variance pathway to fit; whatever the cause, the added variance widens the interval that a benefit must clear, which checklist item 10 generalizes.

\section{What the setup can detect: a planted-signal test}
\label{app:floor}

The simulation in Appendix~\ref{app:power} says what this pipeline should be able to detect; this appendix measures what it does detect. We add a known graph-dependent component to the real responses at a ladder of sizes and report the smallest one recovered. For each target, the component is the mean response of its protein-interaction neighbors, computed over \emph{training rows only}, scaled so its spread matches the training responses' and multiplied by $\delta$, so $\delta$ reads as a fraction of a response standard deviation. A model that can read the graph can then predict a held-out target from its neighbors. Six ladder steps, two models, four paired seeds each: $48$ training runs, no failures.

Two properties make the number meaningful, and both were fixed before any run. No validation response reaches any injected value, including through the scaling constant, which a software test checks by changing a validation response and requiring the whole injection matrix to stay bit-identical. And one step is a \emph{negative control} in which each target receives some \emph{other} target's neighborhood at the largest $\delta$, which keeps the injected component's size and distribution but destroys its relation to the graph. Rows outside the training and validation sets are left bit-identical, so the sequestered split is untouched.

\paragraph{Do not compare steps by their own intervals.} The intervals in the $\Delta$ column are within one step and paired on the seed, so they are tight, and the $\delta{=}0.10$ step's $+0.0041$ sits outside its neighbors' intervals on both sides. Only a wrong graph removes a benefit that the correct graph provides, which is what the scrambled control does here.

At $\delta{=}0.40$ the gated model carries only $1.7\times$ the untyped model's spread while its estimate is $4.1\times$ smaller, and handing it the untyped model's standard deviation outright still leaves it short ($t{=}2.00$, $p{=}0.14$, $0.84$ corrected over six). The untyped increment is $2.5\times$ the gated one, and that ratio is the whole of the measured difference in sensitivity to the injected signal. ``Passes at $0.02$ against nothing up to $0.40$'' is a statement about levels and should be read as one.

The claim this supports is narrower than blindness to graph structure: \emph{at four paired seeds}, the gated encoder's advantage over no graph passes correction at no injected size up to $0.40$ response SDs, twenty times the size at which the untyped model's does. Our headline null is measured with that instrument, so it describes what this encoder can resolve at this budget, not whether a PPI prior helps.

\paragraph{Two limits, both fixed before the ladder ran.} First, the untyped ladder measures the \emph{best} model's floor. Amendment 6.5 registered the untyped model against the no-graph model rather than the gated model, on cost ($2.3$ to $2.5$ GPU-hours per run against $7.5$ to $16$) and on sensitivity: when the ladder was registered, the untyped model was the only one with a corrected-significant positive, so a floor measured with it bounds the best detector we had, and weaker models need at least as much signal. It says the pipeline can carry a graph effect of $0.02$ response SDs to a detectable comparison; it does \emph{not} say the typed encoder can.

Second, the injected component is the mean response of a target's PPI neighbors, which is, up to scaling, what a message-passing layer computes. The ladder therefore measures sensitivity to a signal shaped like the architecture's own built-in assumption, the most favorable case there is. The scrambled control rules out the models responding to the size of the injection regardless of the graph; it does not rule out that the injected signal has exactly the form this architecture is built to find. A real graph effect in biology need not be a neighbor mean, and a floor measured against one does not transfer to one that is not.

\paragraph{The scrambled damage is measured at one size.} We measured it at a single injection size, $0.40$ response SDs, which is large. Whether it scales down is not settled here, and a second control step at $\delta{=}0.10$ would not settle it: proportional damage would be $-0.0012$ there, against a minimum detectable effect at four seeds of $0.0044$ on this control's own seed spread, $0.0082$ on the injected steps' median, and $0.0080$ once the enlarged ladder family is corrected for. Separating proportional from absent damage needs $25$ seeds on the control's spread and $81$ on the steps', against the four the ladder ran. We report the claim at the size we measured and leave the scaling open; an underpowered step would have produced a null that meant nothing, the mistake our architecture search already made (Appendix~\ref{app:archsearch}).

\section{The negative result under other groups' scores}
\label{app:metrics}

Other groups' results differ from ours in splits and scores at once. The score half can be checked without retraining anything, because every model's per-row predictions for the frozen validation set are stored. We re-scored all four models at all five seeds under five endpoints, scores that other papers report, each checked against the paper that reports it: Pearson-$\Delta$ over all genes, which TxPert reports (Table~\ref{tab:family}'s Pearson-delta is computed on the programs instead); Pearson correlation and mean squared error over each perturbation's top-$20$ differentially expressed genes, which \textsc{gears} reports \citep{roohani2024gears}; the E-distance as scPerturb computes it \citep{peidli2024scperturb}; and the energy distance with plain Euclidean distances. The differentially expressed subset is taken from the \emph{observed} response, never from the prediction.

Two cautions apply to the distance endpoints. First, scPerturb computes its E-distance from squared Euclidean distances, and in that form the statistic reduces algebraically to $2\lVert \bar{X}-\bar{Y}\rVert^2$: a difference of means, blind to a difference in spread. We report it for comparability and treat the plain-Euclidean energy distance as the evidence about distributions. Second, this pipeline predicts one pseudobulk response per (target, condition), so both distances compare the distribution of \emph{responses across held-out perturbations}, never populations of cells; claims about single-cell distributions stay out of reach.

Five endpoints times four comparisons is twenty simultaneous tests, and correcting only within each endpoint and then reporting whichever endpoint was kind is the look-elsewhere error. We fixed the correction in the pre-registration before scoring the split: we compute both, within each endpoint and across all twenty cells, and a claim of survival requires the one across all twenty, Bonferroni and Holm together. Table~\ref{tab:metrics} reports all twenty cells.

Two cautions apply to Table~\ref{tab:bins}. First, the \emph{level} of a correlation within an interval is not comparable across intervals: genes are selected by the same observed response that the correlation is computed against, so range restriction and selection on a noisy statistic both bias it. Only the comparison within an interval is clean, because both models are scored on the identical genes of the identical rows and neither model's prediction enters the selection. Second, the resolution of the binning decides whether the harm is visible at all. At decile resolution it disappears, since the top decile spans $1{,}028$ genes and the twenty damaged ones are diluted to a positive $+0.0064$ that passes nothing; a coarser analysis of the same predictions would have concluded that the graph never hurts.

\section{Glossary}
\label{app:glossary}

\paragraph{Biology.} \emph{T cell}: an immune cell; \emph{CD4$^{+}$} or helper T cells coordinate immune responses by signaling to other cells. \emph{Primary cells}: cells taken directly from a donor, as opposed to a \emph{cell line} grown indefinitely in culture, such as K562 (a leukemia line), RPE1 (retinal pigment epithelial cells) or Jurkat (a T-cell leukemia line). \emph{Stimulation}: activating T cells in culture, mimicking how they respond to an infection; the reference screen measures cells at rest and at two times after it. \emph{Expression}: how much RNA a cell makes from a gene. \emph{Perturb-seq}: an experiment that perturbs genes, usually one per cell, here with CRISPR, and reads out every gene's expression in that cell. \emph{CRISPRi} (interference) knocks a gene down, \emph{CRISPRa} (activation) switches it on, and a knockout disrupts it. \emph{Guide RNA}: the molecule that directs CRISPR to its target gene. \emph{Target}: the perturbed gene. \emph{Response}, or \emph{DE profile}: the change in every gene's expression against unperturbed control cells. \emph{Pseudobulk}: the summed expression of a group of cells. \emph{Paralog}: a gene related to another by duplication, often with a similar sequence. \emph{PPI}: protein-protein interaction. \emph{Protein complex}: proteins that bind together to act as one unit. \emph{Functional association}: a link inferred mostly from indirect evidence, such as shared expression patterns or co-mention in the literature. \emph{Degree}: the number of a protein's interaction partners.

\paragraph{Models and training.} \emph{Graph}: the protein-interaction network given to the model. \emph{Prior}: knowledge given to a model before training, here the graph. \emph{Encoder}: a part of a model that turns its inputs into a vector; unqualified, the graph encoder, which reads the graph. \emph{Embedding}: a vector of numbers that represents an object, such as a protein, for a model. \emph{Message passing}: each protein's internal state is updated from its graph neighbors' states. \emph{Edge type}: the kind of evidence behind an edge (physical binding, shared complex, functional association). \emph{Gate}: a learned weight between $0$ and $1$ that scales one edge's message, so $0$ switches the edge off; a gate below $10^{-3}$ counts as dead. \emph{The four models}: no graph (no message passing), typed (message passing with edge types and every gate fixed at $1$), gated (the same with learned gates) and untyped (a plain graph convolution with no types or gates). \emph{h1, h2a, h2b}: the pre-registered comparisons gated $-$ no graph, typed $-$ no graph and gated $-$ typed; the fourth is untyped $-$ no graph. \emph{Programs}: groups of genes that change together; the models predict and are scored on changes in these, and $K$ is their number. \emph{Row}: one (target, condition) response profile, one example for the model. \emph{Loss}: the number that training lowers, mostly the prediction error on training rows. \emph{Penalty}, or \emph{regularizer}: an extra term added to the loss to favor some property, such as few active edges. \emph{Parameter}: a number in the model that training adjusts. \emph{Epoch}: one pass over the training data. \emph{Checkpoint}: a saved copy of a model during training. \emph{Ablation}: removing one part of a model to measure what it contributes. \emph{Rationale}: the small set of edges that the explanation module picks out for a prediction; its \emph{sufficiency} is how far the prediction from those edges alone sits from the full prediction (smaller is better), and its \emph{necessity} is how much deleting them changes the prediction.

\paragraph{Evaluation and statistics.} \emph{\textsc{systema}}: the correlation between predicted and observed responses after the average training response is subtracted from both, computed on the programs and averaged over held-out profiles. \emph{Pearson-delta}: the same correlation without that subtraction. \emph{Centroid accuracy}: the fraction of held-out profiles whose prediction is closer to their own observed profile than to any other. \emph{E-distance} and \emph{energy distance}: distances between two sets of responses. \emph{Split}, or \emph{fold}: the division of targets into training and validation; the \emph{frozen} split is the one every main result uses, a \emph{re-draw} repeats a split's recipe with a new random seed, and the \emph{sequestered} split is a third set of targets held back and never scored. \emph{Seed}: the random number that fixes one training run's random choices. \emph{Paired}: two models compared on the same seed, so run-to-run noise they share cancels. \emph{Point estimate}: the single best-guess value, here a mean over seeds. \emph{Confidence interval (CI)}: a range of values consistent with the data; one that crosses zero means no detectable difference. \emph{$p$-value}: how often a difference at least this large would arise by chance if there were none; a \emph{raw} $p$ is one before correction. \emph{Bonferroni and Holm corrections}: two ways of adjusting $p$-values when several comparisons are tested at once; we require both. \emph{Family}: a set of comparisons corrected together. \emph{Null}: no detectable difference; in a simulation, a \emph{true null} is a comparison with no real effect. \emph{Bound}: the upper end of a confidence interval, the largest effect the data still allow. \emph{Contrast}: a comparison between two models. \emph{Endpoint}: a score used to judge predictions. \emph{Power}: the chance of detecting a real effect of a given size; a test is \emph{underpowered} when that chance is low, and the \emph{minimum detectable effect} is the smallest effect it reliably detects. \emph{Zero point}: in the planted-signal test, a model's difference from no graph with nothing planted; the \emph{increment} is the difference at a planted size minus the same seed's zero point. \emph{Pre-registration}: a written plan of the comparisons and decision rules, fixed before the runs it covers. \emph{Post hoc}: an analysis chosen after seeing data. \emph{Meta-analysis}: pooling one comparison across datasets; a \emph{fixed-effect} pool assumes one shared true effect, a \emph{random-effects} pool lets it differ between datasets, $I^2$ is the share of the spread between datasets that reflects real differences rather than noise, the $Q$ test asks whether that spread exceeds chance, and the \emph{Hartung-Knapp} adjustment corrects a random-effects interval for having few datasets, usually widening it.
